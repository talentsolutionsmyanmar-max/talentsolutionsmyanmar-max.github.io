Never Run Out, Never Over-Order — The Inventory Balance That Gets Myanmar Workers Promoted

The Story

Ko Thiha manages a small storeroom for a food distribution company near Bayintnaung Market in Yangon. Every month, his boss asks him to order cooking oil, rice, and canned goods. Ko Thiha always orders extra — twenty percent more than last month's sales. His logic: Myanmar's inflation means prices go up, so buying more now saves money later. For six months, this felt smart. Then the rains came. Three pallets of canned fish sat in his storeroom for eleven weeks. The labels faded. Moisture crept in. When the sales team finally tried to move them, two retailers rejected the shipment — the cans looked damaged even though the food inside was fine. Ko Thiha's company lost 1.8 million kyats. His boss did not fire him, but the promotion Ko Thiha had been promised went to Ma Aye, who managed a smaller storeroom but could show exactly how she matched her orders to actual sales data week by week. Ko Thiha learned that fear of running out can be more expensive than actually running out.

The Common Trap

Myanmar workers consistently over-order inventory as a hedge against inflation and supply disruption. This is driven by Catastrophizing — imagining the worst-case scenario of running out and letting that fear dictate every decision. The cultural root cause is deep: in Myanmar's scarcity-driven economy and hierarchical workplaces, running out of stock brings visible, immediate blame from a superior, while the cost of excess inventory is invisible and slow. Workers fear the loud punishment more than the silent loss. The devastating career consequence is that over-ordering workers are seen as reactive, not strategic. When companies like an established Myanmar bank's procurement team or a mobile payments company's operations division hire coordinators, they test for data-driven ordering. Workers who say 'I order extra to be safe' reveal they operate on fear, not analysis. They are passed over every time.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The Three Hidden Costs of Every Unit Sitting on Your Shelf

Every product sitting in your storeroom has three costs you cannot see. First, Capital Cost: the money used to buy that product is money that cannot be used for anything else. In Myanmar's economy with inflation above twenty percent, money sitting as unsold inventory loses value every single day. Second, Space Cost: that shelf space, that floor area, that corner — it has a rent cost whether you notice or not. Every square foot occupied by slow-moving stock is a square foot unavailable for fast-moving profitable products. Third, Risk Cost: damage, expiry, theft, moisture, label fading, fashion changing, customer preferences shifting. The longer a product sits, the more likely it becomes worthless. Here is the process to celebrate: every time you look at a shelf and ask 'how long has this been here and what is it costing us?' you are thinking like a supply chain professional. You do not need to calculate perfectly. You need to ask consistently. The question is the habit. The habit builds the skill. The skill earns the promotion.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
How to Apply Inventory Thinking in a Myanmar Workplace Without Overstepping

In Myanmar's hierarchical workplaces, suggesting that inventory levels are wrong can feel like criticizing your boss's decisions. This fear keeps ninety percent of workers silent even when they see waste every day. Here is how to navigate this without losing face for anyone. Step one: track your own data quietly. Use Google Sheets on your phone. Track three products for two weeks — how much comes in, how much goes out, how much sits. Step two: find one product where the data clearly shows over-ordering — maybe eight weeks of supply when only two weeks are needed. Step three: approach your supervisor with this framing: 'I have been tracking our cooking oil inventory and I noticed we might be able to free up some capital if we adjust our order timing. Would you like to see the numbers?' This framing respects hierarchy by asking permission, protects face by saying 'we' not 'you,' and demonstrates initiative by bringing data instead of opinions. In Myanmar's 2026 economy, bosses are desperate for workers who solve problems without creating confrontation. Be that worker.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open Google Sheets on your phone. Title a new sheet 'Inventory Tracker.' Create five columns: Product Name, Starting Quantity Monday, Quantity Used This Week, Ending Quantity Friday, Days of Supply Left. For 'Days of Supply Left,' divide Ending Quantity by the average daily usage (Quantity Used divided by 5 workdays). Track just three products this week. This single sheet will make you more data-driven than ninety percent of storeroom workers in Myanmar. The act of tracking changes your behavior before the data even teaches you anything.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Pick the product you order most frequently at work. On your phone calculator, do this math: Average units sold per day (estimate from last month) multiplied by the number of days it takes your supplier to deliver after you order. That number is your Reorder Point — the stock level at which you should order, not before. Write it down. Compare it to when you actually order. Most workers discover they order far too early or with no trigger at all. Knowing your reorder point is the single most valuable number in inventory management.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Search YouTube for 'what is safety stock simple explanation' and watch one video under eight minutes. As you watch, write in your notes app: 'My current safety stock for [product] is ___ units. This video says it should be calculated as ___.' If there is a gap, you have found a concrete improvement you can bring to your supervisor — not as a complaint, but as a suggestion with numbers. Workers who bring numbers earn trust.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Open your phone's camera. Take a photo of one shelf or storage area at work (or a similar one at home if you cannot photograph work). Look at the photo and count: how many items have been sitting there more than four weeks? For each one, estimate the purchase cost. Add them up. This is your 'dead money' — capital that could be earning interest or buying faster-moving products. Show this number to yourself honestly. This is what over-ordering actually costs. Most people have never calculated it. You just did.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Open WhatsApp and create a group called 'My Inventory Notes' with only yourself in it. Every day this week, send one message to this group at the end of your shift: what product moved fastest, what product did not move at all, and what surprised you. After five days, read all five messages together. You will see patterns that are invisible day-to-day. This is the simplest data collection system possible — free, instant, and on the device you already carry. Celebrate the daily habit, not the perfection of the data.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

Ordering extra feels like safety, but excess inventory is not protection — it is money slowly dying on your shelf. The real safety is knowing your numbers so precisely that you order exactly what you need, exactly when you need it.
