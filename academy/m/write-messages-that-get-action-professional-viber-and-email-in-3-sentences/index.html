The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Zaw Htet is a 28-year-old sales coordinator at a construction materials distributor in South Okkalapa. Every day he sends at least twenty Viber messages to his manager, warehouse team, and clients. Last month his manager screenshotted one of his messages and showed it in a team meeting as an example of 'what not to send.' The message was eleven lines long, had no clear request, mixed three different topics, and ended with a sticker of a cartoon bear bowing. Ko Zaw Htet wanted to disappear. His manager said: 'I read this three times and still do not know what you want me to do.' The real damage came later. A Korean client emailed asking for a quotation update. Ko Zaw Htet replied with a one-line email: 'Dear Sir, I will send you soon.' No subject line. No timeline. No details. The client forwarded it to Ko Zaw Htet's director with a note: 'Is this your standard of communication?' Ko Zaw Htet was removed from client-facing work. His salary stayed flat while colleagues who wrote clear messages got moved to the export team earning forty percent more.

The flipCard 2 of 12
What do you already believe?

When you write a work message on Viber or email, what do you think makes it 'professional'? Do you believe longer messages show more effort and respect? Do you believe using formal English words makes you sound more competent? Think about your honest answer before continuing. Many Myanmar workers confuse length and formality with professionalism — and this belief is silently damaging their careers.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The number one mistake is writing messages with no clear action request — long paragraphs that describe a situation but never state what the reader should do. The cognitive distortion is Emotional Reasoning: because the writer feels urgency or stress, they assume the reader will automatically feel the same urgency and figure out the needed action. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's indirect communication norm, where directly telling someone what to do — especially a senior person — feels rude. The devastating consequence: your messages get ignored. Not because people dislike you, but because busy managers triage by clarity. Unclear messages go to the bottom of the pile. Over months, you become the person whose messages nobody reads carefully. Your requests get delayed, your projects stall, and your performance reviews say 'poor communication skills.'

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The three-sentence rule that changes everything

Here is a truth that will feel uncomfortable: nobody reads your long messages. Not your manager, not your client, not your colleague. Behavioral research shows that the average professional decides within seven seconds whether to act on a message or skip it. Morgan Housel's insight applies here: success in communication is not about talent with words — it is about the discipline of brevity. The cognitive distortion to break is Emotional Reasoning — you feel like you need to explain everything, so you assume the reader needs to hear everything. They do not. They need three things: Context (what is this about), Action (what do you need from me), and Deadline (by when). That is the CAD formula. Every professional message you write for the rest of your career should fit in three sentences using CAD. This is not lazy — this is respectful of the reader's time. It signals that you can think clearly, which is the single most valued communication skill in any workplace.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Adapting CAD for Myanmar work culture: respect without rambling

In Myanmar culture, being direct with a senior person can feel disrespectful. You might worry that a three-sentence message to your manager looks lazy or rude. Here is the adaptation: add a one-line respect opener before your CAD structure. For Myanmar managers, start with a brief polite line. For example, in Viber to your manager: 'Good morning, hope you are well. Regarding the monthly inventory count, may I request your approval on the updated form I sent yesterday? I would need to submit it by Wednesday.' That is a respect opener plus three-sentence CAD. For emails to foreign clients or MNC managers at companies like a major conglomerate or Total Energies, skip the long opener — they prefer you get to the point immediately. The key insight: different audiences need different amounts of softening, but every audience needs the same clarity underneath. Practice writing one message each way today. Notice that both versions have the same CAD core. The structure never changes — only the wrapping does.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open Viber or WhatsApp right now. Scroll to the last work-related message you sent to your manager or a colleague. Read it as if you are the receiver seeing it for the first time. Ask yourself: can the reader tell in five seconds what I want them to do and by when? If not, rewrite it in your Notes app using this formula: Sentence 1 is context — what this is about. Sentence 2 is the action — what you need from them. Sentence 3 is the deadline — by when. This is the CAD formula. Save this rewrite. You have just created your first professional message template.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open Google Docs on your phone — it works on 3G and saves automatically. Create a new document titled 'My Message Templates.' Write three templates using the CAD formula. Template 1: Requesting approval. Example: 'Regarding [topic], I have prepared [document]. Could you please review and approve by [date]?' Template 2: Status update. Template 3: Reporting a problem. Keep each template to three sentences maximum. Having these saved means you never start from a blank screen again — you start from a structure that works.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Go to your email app — Gmail works best on Android. Open your Sent folder. Find the last email you sent for work. Check: does it have a specific subject line that tells the reader what this email is about? Most Myanmar workers leave the subject line blank or write 'Hello.' Rewrite the subject line in this format: '[Action needed] Topic — Deadline.' For example: '[Please approve] October sales report — by Friday Nov 1.' This single habit will make your emails get opened and responded to faster than ninety percent of your colleagues' emails.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Install Grammarly Keyboard from the Google Play Store. It is free and works on 3G. Go to Settings, then Languages and Input, then set Grammarly as your keyboard. Now every message you type in Viber, WhatsApp, or Gmail will be checked for basic grammar and spelling. This is not about perfect English — it is about catching embarrassing mistakes before your manager sees them. Test it right now by opening your Notes app and typing a practice work message.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Find one trusted colleague on Viber. Send them this message: 'I am practicing writing clearer work messages. For the next week, can you tell me honestly if my messages are clear? Just reply Clear or Unclear.' This creates an accountability partner. You are celebrating the process of improving — not waiting for perfection. Even if your colleague says Unclear, that feedback is a gift. You will improve faster in one week with feedback than in one year without it.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

Professional communication is not about writing more or writing in perfect English — it is about making it impossible for the reader to misunderstand what you want them to do and by when.
