Using Competing Offers as Leverage

CORE INSIGHT

A competing offer is not a weapon. It is proof. When you tell an employer you have another offer, you are not threatening them -- you are showing them that the market has validated your skills at a specific price. This is the single most powerful position a Myanmar professional can be in during a salary negotiation, because it removes the guesswork. The employer no longer has to guess your value; another company has already calculated it.

Most Myanmar professionals get this wrong in one of two directions. Some never mention the competing offer because they feel it is disrespectful -- as if loyalty means accepting whatever is given without question. Others blurt it out like a threat: 'Match this or I leave.' Both approaches fail. The first leaves money and opportunity on the table. The second damages trust in a market where your reputation follows you across industries for years.

The approach that works is simple and respectful: share the facts, express genuine interest, and give the employer space to respond. You might say: 'I want to be transparent with you. I have received another offer at a higher salary. I genuinely prefer to stay here because of the team and the growth path, but I want to discuss whether we can close the gap.' This framing does three things -- it shows honesty, signals loyalty, and gives the employer a concrete number to work with.

THE MYANMAR REALITY

In Myanmar's 2026 job market, competing offers carry different weight depending on the employer type. MNCs like Golden Beverage Co., Lotus Consumer Goods, or Jade Ride typically have structured pay bands -- they may not match your offer exactly, but they can often adjust within a range or add benefits like housing allowance or training budgets. Local companies, especially family-owned businesses, negotiate more personally -- the decision often rests with one person, usually the owner or MD, and the conversation is more relational than transactional. NGOs and INGOs have rigid salary scales tied to donor budgets, so competing offers rarely change their number, but they may accelerate your grade level or add hardship allowances.

Culturally, many Myanmar professionals -- especially women and younger employees -- feel deeply uncomfortable telling a boss they have another offer. There is a fear of appearing disloyal or greedy. In reality, most experienced Myanmar hiring managers expect this. They know the market is competitive. What they do not respect is dishonesty -- claiming an offer you do not have, inflating the number, or using it as a last-minute ambush during your resignation.

COMMON MISTAKE

The most damaging mistake is fabricating or exaggerating a competing offer. Myanmar's professional community, especially in Yangon, is remarkably small. HR managers at competitor companies often know each other, attend the same SHRM Myanmar events, and sometimes verify candidates informally. If you claim Company X offered you 1,200,000 MMK when they offered 900,000 MMK -- or worse, when they never made an offer at all -- it will surface. Not always immediately, but eventually. And when it does, your credibility is destroyed not just with that employer but across their network. In a market where referral hiring dominates, this is career poison.

KEY TAKEAWAY

A competing offer earns you the right to a real conversation -- not the right to make demands. Use it honestly, use it respectfully, and let the employer decide what you are worth to them with full information.
