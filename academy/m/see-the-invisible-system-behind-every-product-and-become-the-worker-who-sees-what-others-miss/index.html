The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Hla Hla works at a distribution warehouse in Hlaing Tharyar. Every morning she checks goods in, writes quantities on paper, and stacks boxes. One Thursday, a a major multinational based in Yangon regional buyer visited and asked her one question: 'This shipment of cooking oil — can you tell me how many days it took from the factory to this shelf, and where it waited longest?' Ma Hla Hla froze. She had touched that cooking oil every day for two years. She knew the brand, the weight, the price. But she had never once thought about the journey. She could not answer. The buyer nodded politely and moved on. That afternoon, Ma Hla Hla's supervisor told her the company was looking for someone to promote to inventory coordinator. The buyer's visit was an informal assessment. Ma Hla Hla was passed over for Ko Zaw, who had only worked there for eight months but could trace any product's journey on a whiteboard. Ma Hla Hla earns the same salary today. Ko Zaw earns forty percent more.

What do you already believe?

Think honestly: what is supply chain management? If someone asked you at a job interview, what would you say? Most workers say 'it is about transporting and storing goods.' Before we teach you anything, write your answer on your phone's notes app. Be honest — your first answer is the starting point, not a test.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

Most Myanmar supply chain workers believe their job is only the physical task in front of them — receiving, stacking, counting, loading. This is Tunnel Vision, a cognitive distortion where you see only your narrow piece and assume the whole picture does not concern you. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's strict workplace hierarchy: junior workers are trained to do what they are told and never ask 'why does this process work this way?' The devastating consequence is permanent career stagnation. When MNC employers like a major multinational based in Yangon or a major conglomerate hire for coordinator and supervisor roles, they specifically test whether candidates can describe the end-to-end flow. Workers who cannot are filtered out in the first interview round, regardless of years of experience. Ten years of 'just stacking boxes' counts as one year of experience repeated ten times.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Supply Chain Is a System, Not a Series of Tasks

A supply chain is every step a product takes from raw material to the customer's hands — and critically, the information and money that flow backward at each step. When a customer at Fresh Market Chain buys a bottle of fish sauce, that triggers a chain: the store orders more from the distributor, the distributor orders from the factory, the factory orders raw materials. Supply chain management means making this entire chain faster, cheaper, and more reliable. Here is the perspective shift: your job is not your task. Your job is your task's relationship to every other task in the chain. When you receive goods slowly, downstream delivery is late. When you count inventory wrong, the purchasing team orders too much or too little. The worker who sees only their task is a pair of hands. The worker who sees the system is a brain. MNC employers pay for brains. Celebrate this: every time you ask 'what happens before and after my step?' you are building the system-thinking habit that separates coordinators from laborers. Process over outcome — the habit of asking matters more than getting the perfect answer today.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Applying System Thinking in a Myanmar Workplace

In many Myanmar companies, departments do not share information. The purchasing team does not tell the warehouse what is coming. The warehouse does not tell sales what is running low. This silence is partly cultural — junior staff feel it is not their place to ask other departments questions, and senior staff guard information as power. But supply chain only works when information flows freely. Here is what you can do without breaking hierarchy: start by mapping your own step perfectly. Know your numbers — how many units come in, how long they stay, how many go out, what gets damaged. Write this in a simple Google Sheet. When your supervisor asks a question, you will be the only person with data. That is how you earn trust to ask about other steps. In Myanmar's 2026 economy, companies that survive inflation and disruption will be the ones where workers connect information across steps. You do not need permission to observe. You do not need a title to think in systems. Start with your step, document it, and let the data speak for you.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open Google Sheets on your phone (free with any Gmail account, works on 3G). Create a new sheet titled 'My Workplace Supply Chain Map.' In Column A, write these five rows: Supplier, Transport In, Warehouse, Transport Out, Customer. In Column B, write the name of the actual company or person at each step for one product you handle at work. In Column C, estimate how many days the product spends at each step. You now have your first supply chain map. Save it. You will use this in every future module.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Go to YouTube and search 'supply chain explained simple' — watch the first video under ten minutes. As you watch, pause every time they mention a step. On your phone's notes app, write whether your workplace does that step well or poorly. This trains you to compare global standards against your reality. Do not just watch passively — pause and write. The writing is the learning.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open Facebook and search for the group 'Myanmar Logistics and Supply Chain Professionals.' Request to join. Once inside, read the five most recent posts. Notice what problems people discuss — late deliveries? Damaged goods? High costs? Write down the three most common problems you see. These are the problems your industry pays people to solve. If you can solve them, you become valuable.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Pick one product you handled at work today. Open WhatsApp and send yourself a voice note answering these questions: Where did this product come from? How did it get here? How long did it wait? Where does it go next? Who pays if it gets damaged or delayed? If you cannot answer all five, that is the gap between where you are and where a supply chain coordinator needs to be. Replay your voice note tomorrow morning before work.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Open the calculator app on your phone. Take one product your warehouse handles. Estimate: purchase price per unit, transport cost to reach your warehouse (ask a colleague or guess), and how many days it sits before being sold or shipped. Multiply the number of waiting days by 500 kyats (a rough daily storage cost estimate). Add that to transport cost. This rough total is the 'landed cost' — the real cost, not just the purchase price. Most workers have never calculated this. You just did.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

Supply chain is not about touching goods — it is about seeing the invisible connections between time, money, and movement that everyone else ignores. The worker who sees the system gets promoted; the worker who only sees the box stays where they are.
