Understanding the Myanmar CV Format

Ma Hnin Si graduated from Dagon University two years ago. She has been working as an admin assistant at a construction company in Hlaing Tharyar, earning 280,000 MMK per month. She applied to 47 jobs on JobNet last month. She got one interview call. Her CV was three pages long, started with her father's name and NRC number, included a casual selfie, and listed her high school achievements before her work experience. She could not understand why employers were not responding. The problem was not her experience. The problem was her CV.

Here is the core insight that changes everything: your CV is not a biography. It is a 15-second sales document. Hiring managers at Myanmar companies -- whether a local trading firm, an NGO like Save the Children, or an MNC like Golden Beverage Co. -- spend an average of 10 to 20 seconds on a first-pass CV screen. If they cannot find your job title, your relevant experience, and your contact information within that window, your CV goes into the reject pile. It does not matter how qualified you are.

Most Myanmar professionals treat the CV as a comprehensive personal record. They include everything: father's name, religion, marital status, NRC number, height, weight, hobbies like 'reading and traveling,' and a passport-style photo taken at a studio five years ago. They do this because that is what they saw their seniors do, and because some Myanmar job application forms still ask for these details. But the CV and the application form are different documents. Your CV should contain only what helps an employer decide to interview you -- nothing more.

The correct Myanmar CV format for 2026 follows this order. At the top: your full name, phone number (the one with Viber -- employers will Viber you before they call), email address, and city. Not your full home address. Below that: a professional photo -- shoulders up, plain background, taken within the last year. This photo requirement is real in Myanmar; unlike Western markets where photos are discouraged, every Myanmar employer expects one, and not including it actually creates suspicion. Next: your work experience in reverse chronological order, meaning your most recent job first. For each role, include the company name, your title, the dates, and two to four bullet points describing what you accomplished -- not just your duties. Then education, then skills. One to two pages for most professionals. Only senior managers with 10-plus years need a third page.

In Myanmar specifically, the CV format expectations differ significantly by employer type. Local Myanmar companies, especially in manufacturing, trading, and construction, still sometimes expect the old-style CV with personal details and references listed at the bottom. NGOs and international organizations strongly prefer the clean, achievement-focused format and will quietly reject CVs that look outdated. MNCs operating in Myanmar follow global standards entirely. If you are applying across these employer types, you need two versions of your CV -- one that includes a brief personal details section for traditional employers, and one clean version for international organizations. For roles in Mandalay and other cities outside Yangon, local company expectations lean more traditional, so knowing your target employer matters. In 2026, many employers also run your name through LinkedIn before interviewing you, and some use AI screening tools that parse your CV for keywords. A messy format with images embedded in tables or fancy graphics will break these parsers and your CV will never be seen by a human.

The most common mistake Myanmar professionals make is listing job duties instead of achievements. They write 'Responsible for data entry' instead of 'Processed 200-plus customer records daily with 99 percent accuracy, reducing monthly errors by 30 percent.' The first tells the employer what chair you sat in. The second tells them what you are worth. Most people make this mistake because Myanmar work culture emphasizes role and position over individual contribution -- you were taught to describe where you fit, not what you changed. But employers hire for impact, especially in 2026 when every company is trying to do more with fewer people.
