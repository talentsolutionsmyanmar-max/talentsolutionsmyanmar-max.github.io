Own the Problem and Own Your Career: Why Taking Responsibility Wins Every Time

The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Min Khant works at a phone repair shop near Sule Pagoda in downtown Yangon. A customer brought in a phone that was repaired last week but stopped working again. Ko Min Khant knew the repair was done by his coworker on the previous shift. His first instinct was to say 'That was not my work. The other technician did it. Come back when he is here.' The customer's face hardened. She said 'This is your shop, is it not?' and walked out. She posted on the shop's Facebook page that evening. Twelve people reacted with angry faces. The shop owner saw the post before Ko Min Khant did. The next morning, Ko Min Khant was told he would not be given the senior technician promotion he had been waiting for. The owner said: 'A senior technician owns every problem that walks through the door, not just the ones with his name on them.' Ko Min Khant lost a forty percent salary increase because of five words: 'That was not my work.'

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The number one mistake is deflecting responsibility by pointing to another person, another department, or another shift. The cognitive distortion is Blame Externalization: believing that since you did not cause the problem, you have no obligation to own it. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's strict hierarchy, where workers genuinely believe that problems belong to whoever created them or to the manager above. Stepping into someone else's responsibility space feels like overstepping. The devastating consequence: customers see the entire business as incompetent, and managers see you as someone who avoids difficulty. In MNC interview panels at companies like a major multinational based in Yangon and a major conglomerate, interviewers deliberately ask 'Tell me about a time you dealt with a problem that was not your fault.' Candidates who describe deflection are immediately eliminated.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The ownership paradox: taking responsibility for what you did not cause builds your power

Here is what feels counterintuitive but is proven in every high-performing service organization: the person who takes ownership of problems they did not cause gets promoted faster than the person who only handles their own responsibilities. Why? Because from the customer's perspective, your company is one entity. They do not see departments or shifts. They see one person in front of them who is either helping or deflecting. When you say 'That was someone else,' the customer hears 'Nobody here will help me.' When you say 'Let me take care of this for you,' the customer hears 'I am safe. Someone competent is in charge.' MJ DeMarco's framework reveals the deeper truth: employees who think like business owners, taking responsibility for the whole customer experience, are the ones who eventually become managers, then business owners themselves. This is not about being a doormat. You are not accepting blame. You are accepting the role of problem-solver. That role is the most powerful position in any organization.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Navigating ownership in Myanmar's hierarchy without stepping on toes

In Myanmar workplaces, there is a real concern: if you take ownership of a problem caused by a senior person or a colleague on another shift, will they see it as you overstepping? Will your manager think you are being disrespectful to the hierarchy? Here is how to handle this with cultural intelligence. First, separate the customer-facing action from the internal communication. To the customer, you always say: 'I will take care of this.' To your colleague or manager afterward, you say: 'A customer came in with an issue from the previous shift. I went ahead and resolved it for them. Just wanted to let you know in case you need the details.' This approach respects hierarchy while serving the customer. You are not accusing anyone. You are informing with respect. At an established Myanmar bank branches and a major multinational based in Yangon stores, team leads actively identify workers who handle cross-shift and cross-department issues smoothly. These workers get assigned to higher-value positions. Start this week: the next time a customer brings someone else's problem, resist the deflection instinct. Use Own-Explain-Act. Then respectfully inform the relevant colleague afterward. You protect both the customer and the relationship.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your phone's notes app. Write down the three phrases you most commonly use when a customer brings a problem that is not your fault. Be brutally honest. Examples: 'That is not my department.' 'The other person did that.' 'You need to come back later.' Now rewrite each phrase using the Own-Explain-Act framework. Own: 'I am sorry you are dealing with this.' Explain: 'Let me find out exactly what happened.' Act: 'I will personally make sure this gets resolved for you today.' Save these rewrites and read them aloud twice.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open YouTube and search for 'taking ownership customer service examples.' Watch one video under five minutes. Pay attention to the moment the worker says 'I will take care of this' even when the problem was clearly caused by someone else. Notice the customer's reaction. In your notes app, write one sentence about what you observed. This visual proof rewires your belief that ownership means accepting blame. Ownership means accepting responsibility for the solution, not the cause.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open your voice recorder. Imagine this scenario: a customer says 'I was told my order would arrive yesterday and it did not. This is the second time.' Record yourself responding with the Own-Explain-Act framework. Play it back. Does your voice sound defensive? Does it sound like you are making excuses? Record it again until it sounds calm, confident, and genuinely caring. This practice session costs nothing and prepares you for the real moment.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Send a message to one coworker on Viber or Messenger with this question: 'When a customer brings a problem that someone else caused, what do you usually say?' Read their response. Then share your new Own-Explain-Act approach. This creates an accountability partner at work. When both of you practice ownership, customers experience a more professional team. Taleb's asymmetric thinking applies: one small conversation creates a systemic improvement across your workplace.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Set a phone reminder for the end of each shift this week with the text: 'Did any customer bring a problem today that was not my fault? How did I respond?' In your notes app, write one honest sentence each day. Track whether you deflected or owned. Do not judge yourself harshly. Duke's process principle says celebrate the act of tracking itself. By Friday, you will see your own pattern clearly and know exactly where to improve.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

The customer does not care whose fault it is. They care who is going to fix it. The moment you take ownership of a problem you did not create, you become the most trusted person in the room.
