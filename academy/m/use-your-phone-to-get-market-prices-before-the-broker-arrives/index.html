The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Thida grows green gram on three acres near Meiktila in the Dry Zone. Every harvest, she waits for Ko Aung, the local broker, to drive his truck to the village and announce his buying price. She has no other information source. In February 2025, Ko Aung offered 58,000 kyat per basket. Ma Thida sold everything that day — she needed cash for her daughter's school fees. Two weeks later, she saw on a neighbor's Facebook feed that green gram at Mandalay Zay Cho wholesale market was selling at 78,000 per basket on the exact day she sold. The 20,000 kyat per basket difference across her entire harvest was over 600,000 kyat — enough to cover three months of her family's food. Ma Thida did not lose that money because green gram prices dropped. She lost it because she had no price information at the moment of the transaction. The broker did not lie to her. He simply had information she did not.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

Myanmar farmers commonly believe that the broker's offered price reflects the real market price minus fair transportation and handling costs. The cognitive distortion is the Authority Bias — because the broker travels to the city, drives a truck, and speaks confidently about market conditions, farmers grant them expert authority over price. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's deep hierarchy respect: the broker is perceived as higher-status because of mobility, capital, and connections, making it culturally difficult to question their price. The devastating consequence is systematic value extraction — studies show Myanmar smallholders receive only 40-55% of the final wholesale price, with the information-holder capturing the rest as margin, not as legitimate service cost.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Why information is the cheapest investment with the highest return

Nassim Taleb teaches a principle called asymmetric opportunity: a small action with minimal cost that can produce a large positive outcome. Checking crop prices on your phone costs you zero kyat and 2 minutes. But it can change your selling price by 10-30%. No fertilizer, no seed variety, no farming technique in the world offers that return for that cost. The cognitive trap you must escape is what psychologists call Learned Helplessness — the belief that because you have always accepted the broker's price, that is the only option. This distortion thrives on information darkness. The moment you see actual wholesale prices, the helplessness breaks because now you have evidence. You do not need to become a trader. You do not need to bypass the broker entirely. You simply need to know the number before the negotiation begins. Process over outcome (Duke's principle): your goal is not to win every negotiation. Your goal is to check the price every single time. Build the habit. The results follow the process.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
How to read price information without being overwhelmed

Many Myanmar farmers tell us they tried checking prices online but felt confused by conflicting numbers, different units, and unfamiliar market names. This is normal. Here is how to make it simple for Myanmar context: First, identify your reference market — for most crops this is Bayintnaung Market in Yangon or Zay Cho in Mandalay. Second, check the unit: prices may be listed per basket, per viss, or per metric ton. Convert to whatever unit you normally sell in. Your phone calculator handles this. Third, understand that wholesale market price is always higher than what you receive at the farm gate — a 15-20% gap for transport and handling is normal and fair. Anything beyond 25% gap means the broker is taking excess margin. In Myanmar culture, you do not need to confront directly. Simply telling the broker 'I saw Bayintnaung price is X, can you help me understand the gap?' is polite, respectful, and devastatingly effective. You maintain face for both parties while establishing that you are informed.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open Facebook on your phone and search for these specific groups: 'Bayintnaung Wholesale Market Prices' and 'Myanmar Pulses Traders Association.' Also search for your specific crop name in Myanmar — for example 'ပဲတီစိမ်း ဈေးနှုန်း' for green gram prices. Join every relevant group you find. Turn on notifications for these groups so price updates appear automatically. This takes 5 minutes and immediately closes the information gap between you and any broker.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open your phone browser and go to the website of Myanmar Agribusiness Public Corporation (MAPCO) or search 'MAPCO Myanmar crop prices.' Also try searching 'Department of Trade Myanmar commodity prices.' Bookmark any page that shows daily or weekly crop prices. Not every site works perfectly on 3G, so try at least three sources and keep the ones that load fastest on your connection.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Create a simple price tracking system: open your phone's Notes app and create a note titled with your crop name and the current month. Every 2-3 days, check your Facebook groups or bookmarked sites and write the date and price. After 2 weeks you will see a pattern — prices rise and fall with supply cycles. This 30-second habit gives you what brokers charge millions for: market timing intelligence. Even imperfect data destroys the broker's information monopoly.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Open WhatsApp or Viber and create a group called 'Our Crop Prices' with 3-5 farmers you know who grow similar crops. Each person commits to posting one price update per week from whatever source they find. Five farmers checking different sources means five times more information flowing to everyone. This is how trading companies work — they pool information. Farmers can do exactly the same thing for free.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

The next time any broker offers you a price, before saying yes or no, say: 'Let me check something.' Open your phone in front of them and look at your price notes or Facebook group. You do not need to argue or confront. Simply looking at your phone in that moment communicates that you are an informed seller. Watch how the conversation changes. This small behavior shift — checking before accepting — is worth more than any farming technique.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

In agriculture, information asymmetry is the most expensive cost you will never see on any invoice — the farmer who checks market prices on their phone before selling earns more than the farmer who buys better fertilizer.
