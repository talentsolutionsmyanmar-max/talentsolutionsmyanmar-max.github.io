Present Your Design Decisions So Clients Say Yes on the First Review

The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Aung Kyaw Moe is 31 and has been doing design work in Yangon for five years. Last month a tea brand in Lanmadaw hired him to design their new Facebook campaign. He spent two days on the designs and sent them via Viber with one message: Here are the designs. The client responded: I do not like the color. Change it. Ko Aung Kyaw Moe changed it. Then: The font feels wrong. He changed it. Then: Can you try a completely different direction? After six revision rounds spanning eleven days, the client paid him half the original agreed price, saying the project took too long. Ko Aung Kyaw Moe was furious and exhausted but said nothing because he did not want to damage the relationship. What he never realized was that the problem started with his very first Viber message. He sent designs without context. He never explained why he chose those colors, that font, or that layout. He forced the client to become the designer — judging every detail based on personal taste rather than strategy. He handed over his professional authority in a single message.

The Common Trap

Myanmar designers almost always send completed designs to clients with no explanation — just the image file with a brief message like Please review. They then receive subjective feedback based on personal taste rather than business logic, leading to endless revision cycles. The cognitive distortion is Personalization — they interpret client criticism of the design as criticism of themselves, so they avoid discussion and simply comply with every change request silently. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's hierarchy norm where the client is perceived as the superior authority figure and the designer's role is to execute orders, not to guide decisions. The devastating consequence is a permanent loss of professional authority — the designer becomes an order-taker rather than a valued advisor, commanding low rates and attracting only price-sensitive clients who will never pay premium fees.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Rationale Transforms Order-Takers into Trusted Advisors

There are two types of designers in every market. The order-taker sends files and waits to be told what to change. The advisor presents solutions and guides the client toward the best decision. The difference is not talent — it is the three-sentence rationale. When you explain why you chose a specific color, you are not being arrogant. You are doing your professional duty. You are translating visual decisions into business language that the client can evaluate rationally rather than emotionally. This is Morgan Housel's core insight applied to creative work: the behavior of presenting rationale — consistently, every single time — matters more than being the most talented designer in Yangon. Consider the asymmetry that Taleb teaches: the upside of providing rationale is potentially eliminating three to five revision rounds and establishing yourself as a premium professional. The downside is spending five extra minutes writing three sentences. That is a massively favorable trade. Make the rationale a non-negotiable step in your process, regardless of client size.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Navigating Client Feedback in Myanmar's Face-Saving Culture

Myanmar's communication norms create a specific trap for designers. When a senior client says I do not like this, the cultural instinct is to immediately comply and change everything without discussion. But immediate compliance without understanding the real concern leads to blind guessing — which leads to even more revision rounds. The solution is indirect professional redirection. Never say the client is wrong. Instead use the bridge phrase: I appreciate your feedback — the reason I approached it this way was [rationale]. Would you prefer to explore a different direction that still achieves [business goal]? This structure respects hierarchy while maintaining your professional value. Send your rationale in writing via Viber rather than verbally — written communication gives both parties face-saving space to reconsider positions without confrontation. One Myanmar-specific tip: when presenting to a panel or group of decision-makers which is common in local companies, identify the most senior person and address your rationale to their business concern first. Others in the group will follow their lead. Understanding this panel dynamic is essential for design presentations at companies like a major conglomerate or Fresh Market Chain where multiple stakeholders review creative work.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your notes app and think about a design you recently completed or one you are working on. Write three sentences using this exact structure: Sentence one — I chose this color because [link to audience emotion or brand identity]. Sentence two — I selected this font because [link to readability or brand personality]. Sentence three — The layout emphasizes [the main element] because [that is what drives the business goal]. This is your design rationale. Practice writing rationales for three different designs today until the format feels natural.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Create a Canva presentation template you will reuse for every client delivery. Open Canva and select Presentation. Slide one shows the design in a realistic mockup — a phone screen frame for social media or a signboard photo for print. Slide two lists the brief summary in three bullets: audience, goal, mood. Slide three shows the design itself at full size. Slide four contains your three-sentence rationale. Save this as a template. Export as PDF — it loads faster on 3G than multiple image files.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Practice the redirect phrase right now by typing it into your notes app: I understand your concern about [specific element]. The reason I chose this approach is that [business rationale]. Would you like me to explore an alternative that still achieves [the business goal]? This phrase does three things: it validates the client's feelings which preserves face, it reasserts your professional reasoning, and it keeps the conversation anchored to the business objective rather than personal taste. Memorize this structure. Use it in your next client conversation.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Open Facebook and find a design you admire from any brand page. Screenshot it. Now open your notes app and write a two-sentence rationale as if you were the designer explaining your choices to the client. Why those colors? Why that layout? Why that font size? Do this reverse-rationale exercise for three different brand posts. This builds the analytical muscle of articulating design decisions — the exact skill that separates a 5,000-kyat designer from a professional creative consultant.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

The next time you deliver a design to any client — even a friend or family member — do not send just the image file. Send your Canva presentation PDF with mockup, brief summary, and rationale. Then add this message: I have designed this based on our brief. Here is my reasoning for the key decisions. Please share your feedback and I am happy to refine any elements that you feel could better serve your business goal. Time how the client responds versus your previous delivery method. Track whether revision rounds decrease.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

You are not sending a design for judgment — you are presenting a solution to a problem. When you explain the reasoning behind each design choice before asking for feedback, you transform the client from an art critic into a business decision-maker. Rationale is the armor that protects your work.
