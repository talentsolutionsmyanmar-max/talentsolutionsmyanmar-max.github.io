Price Your Design Work Based on Value — Not Hours — and Stop Undercutting Yourself

The Story

Ma Su Myat Mon is 27, living in North Dagon, running a one-person design business from her phone. She designs Facebook posts, Viber catalog images, and shop signage for small businesses. She charges 5,000 kyats per design because she calculated that each one takes about one hour to make. Last month, a growing restaurant chain in Hlaing township asked her to design ten social media posts for their Thingyan promotion. She quoted 50,000 kyats — ten posts times 5,000 each. The restaurant owner agreed instantly, no negotiation. That speed should have been a warning signal, but Ma Su Myat Mon felt proud. She later discovered that those ten posts helped the restaurant generate over 3,000,000 kyats in Thingyan reservations. Her designs — which directly drove that revenue — earned her the equivalent of 1.6 percent of the value she created. She had priced her labor, not her impact. With inflation above twenty percent, her 5,000 kyats per design was worth less each month while the value she delivered to clients kept growing.

The Common Trap

Myanmar designers price exclusively by time or by comparison to other designers' rates, believing that the market determines their value and they must charge what everyone else charges to get work. The cognitive distortion is Anchoring — fixating on the first reference price they encountered, typically 3,000 to 8,000 kyats per design, and never questioning whether it reflects actual value delivered. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's collectivist modesty norm, where asking for significantly more than peers is perceived as arrogant or greedy, and where self-promotion feels deeply uncomfortable. The devastating consequence in Myanmar's inflationary 2026 economy is lethal: if your rate stays at 5,000 kyats while inflation runs above twenty percent, your real income drops every single month. Designers who cannot reframe pricing around value are mathematically guaranteed to become poorer over time, regardless of how hard they work.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The Value Ladder: Why Your Price Must Track Impact, Not Effort

MJ DeMarco's progression framework reveals a critical truth: most designers are stuck in the employment mindset — trading time for money — even when they work as freelancers. True self-employment means pricing based on the value you create, not the hours you spend. Consider two designs that each take one hour. Design A is a personal birthday invitation for a friend — nice, but generates zero revenue. Design B is a Thingyan promotional campaign for a restaurant chain that generates three million kyats in reservations. Should both cost 5,000 kyats because both took one hour? Obviously not. Yet hourly pricing makes them identical. The shift from hourly to value pricing is the single most important financial decision in a creative career. It is also asymmetric in your favor, following Taleb's framework: if you quote higher based on value and the client accepts, your income leaps dramatically. If they negotiate, you can adjust down. If they reject, you learn their budget for next time. The downside of asking for more is almost zero. The downside of never asking is financial erosion every month in Myanmar's inflationary economy.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Raising Prices in Myanmar's Relationship-First Business Culture

Myanmar business culture operates on relationships, and raising prices with existing clients triggers deep anxiety about damaging those relationships. Here is the reality: clients who value you will accept reasonable increases when framed correctly. Clients who leave over a ten percent increase were already undervaluing you. Frame price increases around inflation — every Myanmar business owner understands that costs are rising above twenty percent annually. You can say: As material and operating costs have increased significantly this year, I have adjusted my project rates to continue providing the same quality you expect. This framing is honest and culturally appropriate — it references external forces rather than personal ambition, which avoids the appearance of greed. For new clients, never share your price before asking about their project goals and budget range. The question What is your budget range for this project is not rude — it is standard professional practice that Myanmar MNC marketing departments at companies like an established Myanmar bank and a mobile payments company expect. If you find yourself unable to raise prices at all, that signals a portfolio problem — revisit Module One. Clients pay premium prices to designers whose portfolios demonstrate premium work. The pricing and the portfolio must evolve together.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your notes app and list every client you have worked with in the last three months. For each client, write down: what you charged, how long it took, and your best estimate of what business result your design helped produce — sales, followers gained, event attendance, anything measurable. Divide the business result by what you charged. This ratio reveals how much value you gave away. If a design helped generate 500,000 kyats in sales and you charged 5,000 kyats, you captured one percent of the value. Even capturing five percent would quintuple your income. This exercise should be uncomfortable. That discomfort is the beginning of financial awareness.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Create a three-tier pricing template in your notes app. Tier one — Basic: single standalone design with no revisions included, lowest price. Tier two — Standard: design plus one revision plus brief consultation, middle price. Tier three — Premium: design series plus two revisions plus brief plus social media format variations, highest price. The purpose of three tiers is psychological anchoring — most clients choose the middle option. Set your middle tier at the price you actually want. Set the premium tier thirty percent higher. This is not manipulation — it is professional pricing structure used by every agency worldwide.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Before your next project, ask the client these three value-discovery questions via Viber message: How will you measure whether this design was successful? What revenue or result do you expect from this campaign? How much are you investing in promoting this design through boosted posts or advertising? Their answers reveal the business context. If a client is spending 200,000 kyats on Facebook ad boosting for your design, your 5,000 kyat design fee is absurdly disproportionate. Frame your fee as a percentage of the campaign investment — five to fifteen percent is a reasonable starting range.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Open Google Sheets on your phone — it is free and works offline. Create a spreadsheet with these columns: Month, Number of Projects, Total Income, Average Per Project, and Target Next Month. Fill in the current month's data. Set your target for next month at ten percent higher than current average per project. Review this sheet on the first day of every month. This simple tracking habit ensures you never drift backward. In Myanmar's inflationary economy, a designer who does not actively increase rates is passively accepting a pay cut every single month. The spreadsheet makes this visible and actionable.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Write and save a price communication script on your phone for when clients ask your rate. The script follows this structure: Based on the goals you described for this project and the audience you want to reach, I recommend [tier name] which includes [list deliverables]. The investment is [amount]. This is designed to help you achieve [their stated business goal]. Notice the word investment instead of price or cost. This single word reframes the conversation from expense to business decision. Practice saying this script out loud three times right now — verbal rehearsal makes it feel natural when you need it in a real Viber call or meeting.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

You are not selling hours of mouse-clicking — you are selling business results made visible. A design that drives 3,000,000 kyats in sales is not worth the same as a design that sits unseen. Price the outcome your design enables, not the time it took to create, and you will escape the inflation trap that destroys hourly-rate workers.
