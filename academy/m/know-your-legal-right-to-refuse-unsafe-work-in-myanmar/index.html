The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Thiha is 28 years old and works at a construction materials warehouse near Insein. Last hot season, his supervisor told the team to move chemical drums from a delivery truck into storage without gloves or masks. The drums had no Myanmar-language labels — only Chinese text. Ko Thiha felt dizzy after the first hour. He wanted to stop but he thought: if I refuse, I will be fired during probation. His coworker Ma Su told him the same thing — just finish it, do not make trouble. Ko Thiha kept working. By evening he had chemical burns on both hands. He went to a clinic and paid 45,000 kyat from his own pocket. When he asked his employer to cover it, the supervisor said it was Ko Thiha's fault for not being careful. Ko Thiha did not know that Myanmar's 2013 Factories Act requires employers to provide protective equipment for hazardous materials. He did not know that under Social Security Board rules, his medical costs should have been covered. He suffered because he did not know his rights — and his employer counted on that ignorance.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The number one mistake Myanmar workers make is believing that refusing unsafe work automatically means losing their job. The cognitive distortion is Catastrophizing — jumping to the worst possible outcome without examining evidence. The cultural root cause is the deep Myanmar norm of obedience to authority figures and the economic terror of unemployment during a period of 20 percent plus inflation. Workers imagine immediate termination and blacklisting. In reality, Myanmar labor law provides protections, and MNC employers like a mobile payments company and a major telecom operator have formal safety refusal procedures. The devastating career consequence of not knowing your rights is that you absorb injuries, medical costs, and lost wages that your employer was legally obligated to prevent. You subsidize your employer's negligence with your own body.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
What Myanmar law actually says about your safety at work

The 2013 Factories Act and related Myanmar labor regulations establish clear employer obligations. Employers must provide appropriate protective equipment at no cost to workers for hazardous tasks. Employers must maintain safe working conditions including ventilation, lighting, and structural integrity. Under Social Security Board law, employers must contribute 3 percent of wages and employees 2 percent toward coverage that includes workplace injury medical costs and compensation for lost wages during recovery. Probation under Myanmar law cannot exceed 3 months, and even during probation you retain safety rights. Here is the critical point most workers miss: these are not suggestions to employers. They are legal obligations. When your employer fails to provide safety equipment, they are breaking the law — not you. Nassim Taleb's asymmetric thinking applies here powerfully. Knowing the law costs you nothing. Not knowing the law can cost you your health, your savings, and your family's food security. The asymmetry is extreme. Spend 15 minutes learning your rights today to avoid months of catastrophe tomorrow.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
How to use your rights without destroying your career in Myanmar's hierarchy culture

Knowing your rights and using them wisely are different skills. In Myanmar's workplace culture, direct confrontation with a supervisor causes loss of face for both parties and usually ends badly for the junior person regardless of who is legally correct. The process that works is what safety professionals call Collaborative Escalation. Step one: describe the hazard factually without blame — this is wet, this container has no label, this wire is exposed. Step two: frame your concern as helping the company — I want to help us avoid an incident that could cause problems for the whole team. Step three: if the supervisor dismisses your concern, document it in writing on your phone with date and time. Step four: if the hazard is immediately life-threatening, you have the right to stop work. You do not need permission to avoid dying. For less immediate hazards, written documentation protects you if something goes wrong later. This is not being disloyal. This is being the kind of worker that MNC employers like an established Myanmar bank and a major conglomerate actively seek during hiring — someone who takes ownership of safety rather than passively absorbing risk.

Action 1

Open your phone browser and go to the Myanmar Social Security Board official Facebook page. Search for posts about worker injury claims. Read at least two real cases. Note what documents the workers needed to file their claims: SSB card, medical records, employer incident report. Check right now whether you know where your SSB card is. If you do not have one, write a note to ask your HR department tomorrow. This single document is your financial shield if you are ever injured at work.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open Google Keep or your phone notepad. Type this script and save it with the title Safety Request Script: I have noticed a safety concern at our workstation. I want to help our team stay safe and meet our company's standards. The concern is [describe hazard]. I would like to discuss what we can do together to address it. This script uses collaborative language that respects hierarchy while establishing that you are raising a legitimate issue. Practice reading it aloud twice. The goal is to have these words ready before you need them.

Action 3

Workers who know the law negotiate differently even if they never cite it directly.

Action 4

Open WhatsApp or Viber and find one trusted coworker. Send them this message: I learned today that under Myanmar SSB rules, our employer must contribute 3 percent and we contribute 2 percent for social security including workplace injury coverage. Do you know if our company is doing this? This starts a private conversation that helps both of you verify your coverage. If your employer is not contributing, you now know — and knowledge is the first step toward protection.

Action 5

Key Takeaway

In Myanmar, the workers most likely to be exploited on safety are not the weakest — they are the ones who do not know the law exists to protect them.
