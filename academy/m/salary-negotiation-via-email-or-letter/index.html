Salary Negotiation via Email or Letter

CORE INSIGHT

Email negotiation is not about writing a clever message. It is about removing emotion from a conversation that feels deeply personal. When you negotiate salary face-to-face in Myanmar, cultural pressure to show respect and avoid conflict makes it almost impossible to state your number clearly. Email removes that pressure. You have time to think. You can edit. You can be warm and firm simultaneously -- something that is extremely difficult to do live, especially with a senior HR manager or a hiring director you want to impress.

Here is what most Myanmar professionals get wrong: they treat the email as either a demand or an apology. Some write one aggressive line -- 'I expect 600,000 MMK' -- with no context and no warmth. Others write three paragraphs of apology before whispering their number at the end, buried under phrases like 'if it is not too much trouble' and 'I completely understand if this is not possible.' Both approaches fail. The effective email has exactly four elements: genuine gratitude, a clear number, a brief reason why, and an invitation to discuss. That is it. No apology. No ultimatum.

For example, a Finance Assistant who received an offer of 400,000 MMK from a local construction company wrote back: 'Thank you very much for this offer -- I am genuinely excited about joining the team. Based on my two years of experience with SAP Business One and the current market range I have seen for this role in Yangon (450,000-500,000 MMK), I would like to respectfully request a base salary of 480,000 MMK. I am very open to discussing this and am flexible on start date if that helps.' She received 460,000 MMK -- a 15% increase from the original offer -- within 24 hours.

THE MYANMAR REALITY

In Myanmar's job market, most salary negotiation still happens verbally -- during the interview, on a phone call, or at the offer meeting. Because of this, many candidates do not realize that email negotiation is not only acceptable but often preferred by HR departments, especially at MNCs, international NGOs, and larger local companies like an established Myanmar bank, Fresh Market Chain Group, or Shwe Taung. HR teams actually appreciate written negotiation because it gives them documentation to take to the hiring manager for budget approval. Your email becomes their internal evidence. At smaller local companies where the owner makes salary decisions directly, email can still work but you may need to follow up with a phone call or in-person conversation.

Gender matters here. In Myanmar, women are statistically less likely to negotiate salary at offer stage, partly because of cultural expectations around modesty and partly because of a real fear -- not unfounded -- that pushing back will be seen differently than when a man does it. Email helps level this gap. The written word carries the same weight regardless of who typed it. In 2026, with AI tools like ChatGPT available on most smartphones, you can even draft your email with AI assistance and then personalize it -- there is no shame in this. The skill is knowing what to say, not typing it from memory.

COMMON MISTAKE

The most common mistake Myanmar professionals make is accepting the first offer immediately out of fear. They receive the email, feel relief and excitement, and reply 'I accept' within minutes -- sometimes within seconds. This is understandable. In Myanmar culture, an offer feels like a gift, and refusing a gift feels rude. But an offer is not a gift. It is the starting point of a business conversation. Companies expect negotiation. Most HR managers at reputable companies in Yangon have budget flexibility of 10-20% above the initial offer. When you accept instantly, you are not being polite -- you are leaving money on the table that was already allocated for you. The consequence compounds: your annual raises, your next job's offer, and your lifetime earnings all anchor to this number.

KEY TAKEAWAY

The four-element email -- gratitude, number, reason, invitation to discuss -- takes 15 minutes to write and can be worth millions of kyats over your career. The company already expects you to negotiate; your only job is to do it with warmth and clarity.
