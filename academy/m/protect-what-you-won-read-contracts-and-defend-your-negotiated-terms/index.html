Protect What You Won — Read Contracts and Defend Your Negotiated Terms

The Story

Ma Su Myat is 26 and was hired as a sales coordinator at an FMCG distribution company in Yankin township, Yangon. During the offer discussion, the manager verbally agreed to 480,000 kyats monthly salary with a transportation allowance of 40,000 kyats. Ma Su Myat was thrilled. She signed the employment contract quickly because she did not want to appear distrustful by reading every page in front of the HR officer. When her first payslip arrived, the transportation allowance was missing. She asked HR. They pointed to the contract: it listed only the base salary of 480,000 kyats. No mention of transportation allowance. Ma Su Myat had no written evidence. The verbal promise vanished. She lost 40,000 kyats every month — 480,000 kyats per year — because she was too polite to read a document before signing it. Under Myanmar Labor Law 2013, employment contracts must be written and in a language the worker understands. But the law only protects what the contract says. Everything else is air.

The Common Trap

The most devastating mistake is signing an employment contract without reading it carefully, or reading it but not comparing it against the verbally agreed terms. The cognitive distortion is wishful thinking — believing that because the manager said it, it must be in the contract, and that checking would be unnecessary and offensive. The cultural root cause is that reading a contract line by line in front of the person who drafted it feels like an accusation of dishonesty in Myanmar's trust-based, face-saving culture. Workers feel they must choose between protecting themselves and maintaining the relationship. The consequence is losing negotiated benefits permanently, discovering unfavorable clauses about probation periods, notice requirements, or non-compete terms months or years later when it is too late, and having zero legal recourse because the signed document overrides every verbal promise.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Why the contract is the only negotiation that counts

Everything discussed verbally during a job offer — every promise, every agreement, every assurance from the hiring manager — has exactly zero legal or practical weight unless it appears in the written employment contract. This is not cynicism. This is reality under Myanmar Labor Law 2013, which recognizes written contracts as the governing document of the employment relationship. Verbal promises fail for three common reasons: managers change and new managers do not honor predecessor commitments, company policies get updated and override informal arrangements, and human memory is genuinely unreliable even with good intentions. The process that protects you is simple: after every negotiation conversation, write a summary and send it to the employer via email or message. Then verify every agreed term appears in the contract before signing. This is not paranoia — it is professionalism. Morgan Housel teaches that protecting your downside matters more than maximizing your upside. One missing clause in a contract can cost more than the entire raise you negotiated. The behavior to celebrate is reading carefully, not signing quickly.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
How to request contract changes in Myanmar without appearing distrustful

In Myanmar culture, scrutinizing a contract in front of the person who drafted it can feel like accusing them of dishonesty. This cultural barrier causes thousands of workers to sign unfavorable contracts every year. The solution is to separate the review from the signing. When handed a contract, say: 'Thank you so much. I would like to take this home to read carefully so I can give it the attention it deserves. I will return it signed by [specific date, within 2-3 days].' This is professional, expected at any serious company, and gives you time to review privately without awkwardness. If you find discrepancies between verbal agreements and written terms, address them in writing — a polite WhatsApp message or email — before your next meeting. Written communication about contract terms creates a paper trail and allows the HR person to make corrections without losing face in a live conversation. At MNC employers like a major multinational based in Yangon and an established Myanmar bank, taking time to review contracts is not only accepted but expected. At smaller local companies, framing it as thoroughness rather than suspicion — 'I want to make sure I understand my responsibilities fully' — preserves the relationship while protecting your interests.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open Notes on your phone and create a document titled 'My Contract Review Checklist.' List these items that you must verify in every employment contract before signing: (1) Job title matches discussed role, (2) Base salary matches agreed amount in kyats, (3) All agreed allowances listed separately with amounts — transport, phone, meal, (4) Probation period stated and does not exceed 3 months per Myanmar Labor Law 2013, (5) SSB contribution mentioned with employer 3% and employee 2% breakdown, (6) Working hours and overtime policy clearly stated, (7) Annual leave and sick leave days specified, (8) Notice period for termination by both sides, (9) Annual bonus or performance bonus structure if discussed, (10) Contract language you can fully read and understand. Save this permanently.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

If you currently have an employment contract, find it now. If it is a paper document, photograph every page with your phone camera and save the images in a folder called 'My Employment Documents.' Read through your contract against your new checklist. Mark which items are present and which are missing. Many Myanmar workers have never re-read their contract after signing. You may discover benefits you did not know you had, or missing protections you assumed existed. Knowledge of your current contract is the foundation for any future negotiation.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open your phone browser and search 'Myanmar Labour Law 2013 employment contract requirements.' Read at least one summary article. Key points to note: contracts must be in writing, contracts should be in a language the employee understands (Myanmar language), probation cannot exceed three months, and SSB registration is mandatory. Screenshot the most important legal requirements and save them in your Employment Documents folder. Understanding your legal rights transforms you from a passive signer into an informed party. You do not need a lawyer — you need awareness.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Draft a polite message in your Notes app that you can adapt for any future situation where verbal agreements are not reflected in the written contract. Use this template: 'Thank you for preparing the employment contract. I am very excited to join the team. I noticed that our discussed [specific benefit, e.g., transportation allowance of 40,000 kyats] is not yet reflected in the written agreement. Could we please include this in the contract for clarity and mutual reference? I want to ensure we both have the same understanding documented so there are no misunderstandings in the future.' This message is respectful, references mutual benefit, and is nearly impossible to refuse without revealing bad intent. Save it as a permanent template.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Open Google Sheets on your phone and create a simple document called 'Verbal vs Written Comparison.' Make three columns: Agreed Item, Verbal Promise, and In Written Contract (Yes or No). Before you sign any future contract, fill in this sheet with every term that was discussed during your negotiation. Then read the contract and mark Yes or No for each item. Any 'No' entry becomes a polite discussion point before you sign. This ten-minute process has the potential to save you hundreds of thousands of kyats per year. Taleb's principle applies directly: the small effort of checking creates massive protection against the large loss of missing benefits.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

A negotiation you won verbally but lost in the written contract was never a negotiation you won. The contract is the only reality — everything said before it was a conversation.
