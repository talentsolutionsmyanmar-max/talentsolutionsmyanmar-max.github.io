Build a Hiring Process That Finds Loyal Staff Instead of Chasing Replacements Every 3 Months

The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Thida Aye is an HR officer at a restaurant chain with four branches in Yangon. Every month, she posts the same job ads on Facebook for the same positions: cashiers, servers, kitchen staff. Every month, she receives over 100 applications. She picks the ones with the best-looking CVs, calls them in, asks them to tell me about yourself, and hires whoever seems polite and available. Within three months, more than half quit. Some leave for 10,000 kyats more at another restaurant. Some stop showing up without explanation. Her manager blames her: you keep hiring the wrong people. Ma Thida Aye stays up past midnight rewriting the same Facebook job posts, feeling like she is pouring water into a bucket with no bottom. She has never considered that her hiring process itself is the hole in the bucket. She screens for availability and politeness. She has never screened for alignment, resilience, or realistic job expectations. The people she hires are not wrong. Her process for choosing them is wrong.

The flipCard 2 of 12
What do you already believe?

Before we begin, answer honestly: When someone you hired quits within 3 months, do you believe it is because that person was unreliable, or because your hiring process failed to identify the mismatch? Most Myanmar HR workers blame the person. This belief feels natural but it keeps you trapped in the same cycle. What you believe about why people quit determines whether you can stop it.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The number one mistake is hiring based on gut feeling and surface-level likability rather than structured criteria. The cognitive distortion is the Halo Effect combined with Jumping to Conclusions: this person seems nice and speaks well, so they must be a good worker. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's relationship-oriented society where personal warmth and respectful demeanor are valued above demonstrated competence. In a culture where face-saving prevents tough interview questions, candidates are rarely tested on actual job-relevant skills. The devastating consequence is a revolving door of hires who looked good in a 10-minute unstructured chat but had no realistic understanding of the job, no alignment with the role, and no reason to stay when a marginally better offer appeared.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The 5-Step Hiring Process That Breaks the Turnover Cycle

Step one is writing a realistic job description that includes daily challenges, not just benefits. Step two is posting the job with a realistic preview so that candidates who would hate the reality self-select out before you waste interview time. Step three is asking behavioral questions that reveal past actions, not hypothetical promises. Step four is scoring candidates on a simple rubric of 3 criteria relevant to the role instead of trusting your gut. Step five is giving a realistic first-week overview before the candidate accepts, so their expectations match reality from day one. This process comes from the Duke behavioral principle: celebrate that you followed all five steps in order, regardless of whether the hire works out perfectly. No process guarantees a perfect hire. But a consistent process guarantees that each hire is better informed and better matched than the last. Over time, this compounds. As Morgan Housel teaches, consistency in behavior beats brilliance in talent every time.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Applying Structured Hiring in Myanmar's Relationship-First Culture

In Myanmar, many hires come through referrals from family or existing staff. This is not a bad thing. Referrals can be excellent sources. The problem is when referral means we skip the process. A referred candidate still needs a behavioral interview. They still need a realistic job preview. They still need to be scored on the same rubric as every other candidate. The asymmetric risk from Taleb applies here: the downside of properly interviewing a referred candidate is 30 extra minutes of everyone's time. The downside of skipping the process because a senior person referred them is months of poor performance that nobody can address without causing enormous face-loss to the person who made the referral. In Myanmar companies like a mobile payments company and an established Myanmar bank, referred candidates go through the same structured process as external candidates. This protects the referrer, the candidate, and the company. Frame it to your manager this way: we want to make sure our process sets up referred candidates for success, not just brings them in.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open Facebook on your phone right now. Search for job posts in your industry. Screenshot 3 posts. Look at each one and notice what they promise versus what they say about actual daily work demands. Most posts list benefits and salary but say nothing about challenges, difficult hours, or physical demands. Write in your phone notes what a truly honest version of each post would say. This exercise builds the skill of writing realistic job previews that reduce turnover by filtering out candidates who would quit once they discover the reality.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open Google Docs or your phone notes app. Write 3 behavioral interview questions you could use for any role at your company. Use this formula: Tell me about a specific time when you faced this specific challenge. How did you handle it? What happened? For example: Tell me about a time a customer was angry and you had to handle it alone. What did you do? These questions reveal actual past behavior, which predicts future behavior far better than hypothetical questions like what would you do if a customer was angry. Write all 3 questions now and save them.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Create a simple scoring rubric in your phone notes. Choose 3 criteria that matter most for the role, for example: relevant experience scored 1 to 3, realistic expectations about the job scored 1 to 3, and specific examples given during interview scored 1 to 3. Total score out of 9. Write this rubric now. The next time you interview a candidate, score them on paper or phone instead of deciding by feeling. This single change transforms your hiring from gambling to measuring.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Think about the last person who quit your workplace or a workplace you know. Open your phone notes and write down 3 reasons you believe they left. Now ask yourself honestly: could any of those reasons have been discovered during the hiring process if the right questions were asked? Write yes or no next to each reason. Most Myanmar HR workers discover that at least 2 of 3 reasons were predictable. This exercise proves that better hiring questions directly reduce turnover.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Open Canva on your phone. If you do not have it, download the free version from Play Store. Search for job post templates. Choose one clean template. Customize it for one real position at your workplace or a position you know well. Include these 4 elements that most Myanmar Facebook job posts miss: honest daily tasks, biggest challenge of the role, what success looks like after 3 months, and the exact salary range. Save this as your standard job post template. A professional Canva job post on Facebook immediately signals to candidates that your company is serious and organized.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

High turnover is not a people problem. It is a process problem. The moment you stop blaming workers for leaving and start examining how you chose them, you stop the cycle. A structured 30-minute interview prevents a 30-day replacement search.
