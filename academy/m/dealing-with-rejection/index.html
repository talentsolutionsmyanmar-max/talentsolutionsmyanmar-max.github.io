Dealing with Rejection

Here is what most people never learn about hiring in Myanmar: rejection usually has nothing to do with you. A company posts one accountant position on JobNet. They receive 180 applications. They interview eight people. Seven qualified candidates get rejected -- not because they failed, but because only one chair exists. The HR manager at a Yangon FMCG company recently told us that their hardest part of hiring is saying no to people they genuinely liked. The rejection letter does not mean you were bad. It means someone else was a slightly better fit for that one specific seat on that one specific day.

But here is where most Myanmar professionals get it wrong. They treat rejection as a verdict on their worth. They go quiet. They stop applying for two or three weeks. They tell themselves they need to get another certificate first, or wait for a better time, or that maybe they are not ready. This silence is the real damage -- not the rejection itself. The person who applies to five more jobs the week after a rejection will always outperform the person who pauses to recover for a month. Rejection is not a signal to stop. It is data.

What does that data tell you? It tells you one of three things. First, your CV may not be passing the screening stage -- which means your formatting, keywords, or summary need work, not your qualifications. Second, you may be reaching the interview but not converting -- which means your interview communication needs practice, not more degrees. Third, you may be targeting the wrong level or industry -- applying for senior roles with junior experience, or applying to NGOs with a purely private-sector CV. Each of these is fixable within a week. None of them require you to feel ashamed.

In 2026, AI screening tools are being adopted by larger Myanmar employers and international companies with Yangon offices. Your application may be filtered by software before a human ever reads it. This is not personal rejection -- it is keyword mismatch. Understanding this changes how you respond: you optimize your CV for the system, not just for your pride.

The attitude shift that matters most is this: rejection is not something that happens to you. It is something that happens around you. The market is competitive. Employers have constraints you cannot see -- budget freezes, internal candidates, last-minute role changes. Your job is not to never be rejected. Your job is to stay in motion so that when the right opening appears, you are already there, already sharp, already applying.

Real-World Scenario

Ko Aung Kyaw applied to 12 positions at Gulf Telecom over two years. Rejected every time. On his 13th application, he changed his approach: instead of a generic CV, he wrote a specific cover letter addressing the team's recent project and how his supply chain experience could help. He got the interview. Then the job. The 12 rejections were not failures — they were feedback he was not listening to.

## Common Mistake

In Myanmar culture, rejection carries deep shame. Many job seekers take it personally and either stop applying entirely or blame the system. The truth is that most rejections have nothing to do with your worth as a person. Companies reject candidates because of timing, budget, internal candidates, or simply because someone else was a closer fit for that specific role. The only rejection that matters is the one that makes you stop trying.

## Action Steps

  1. After every rejection, send a polite thank-you email: "Thank you for considering me. I would appreciate any feedback on how I can improve for future opportunities." 30% of hiring managers will respond, and their feedback is gold.
  1. Keep a "rejection journal." For each rejection, write: the company, the role, what you think went wrong, and one thing you will do differently next time. Patterns will emerge.
  1. Set a rule: for every rejection, submit one new application within 48 hours. Momentum defeats despair.

## Reflection Question

Think about the most successful person you know. Ask them how many times they were rejected before their breakthrough. The number will surprise you. Rejection is not the opposite of success — it is the path to it.
