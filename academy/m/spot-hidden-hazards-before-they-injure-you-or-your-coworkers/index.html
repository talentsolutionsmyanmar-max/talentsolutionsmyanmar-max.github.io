The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Hnin Si works on the cutting floor of a garment factory in Hlaing Tharyar. Last rainy season, water leaked through the roof onto the floor near the electrical sockets. She noticed it every morning but said nothing. She assumed the line supervisor already knew. One Tuesday, her coworker Ko Zaw Myo slipped on the wet floor carrying a bundle of fabric and broke his wrist. He lost three months of wages. The factory paid nothing beyond basic SSB coverage. Ma Hnin Si felt sick with guilt. She had seen the hazard for two weeks. She thought reporting it was not her place — she was just a line worker, not a supervisor. What she did not realize was that every worker in that factory had the legal right and the practical duty to flag that puddle. Ko Zaw Myo's broken wrist was not bad luck. It was a predictable injury from a visible hazard that nobody spoke about. This module teaches you to see what everyone else walks past.

The flipCard 2 of 12
What do you already believe?

Think carefully before reading further: whose job is it to identify hazards at your workplace — yours, or your supervisor's? If you see a wet floor or a frayed wire, do you believe it is your responsibility to act, or should you wait for someone with authority? Write your honest answer in your mind. Most Myanmar workers have a clear instinct here, and for most, that instinct is exactly what gets people hurt.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

Myanmar workers overwhelmingly believe that hazard identification is the supervisor's job alone. The cognitive distortion driving this is Personalization Reversal — the belief that because you did not create the hazard, you bear no responsibility for it. The cultural root cause is deep hierarchy respect: in Myanmar workplaces, junior staff believe that speaking about problems above their station is disrespectful or will be seen as criticizing management. The devastating consequence is that hazards persist for days or weeks until someone gets hurt. When an incident investigation happens — especially at MNC employers like a major multinational based in Yangon or a major telecom operator — workers who knew about hazards but stayed silent are viewed as liabilities, not loyal employees. Your silence does not protect you. It marks you as someone who cannot be trusted with responsibility.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Why your brain is designed to ignore familiar danger

Your brain has a feature called habituation. When you see the same hazard every day — the same cracked step, the same dangling wire, the same puddle — your brain stops registering it as dangerous. It becomes background. This is not laziness. It is how human brains conserve energy. But in a workplace, habituation kills. The process that protects you is called active scanning: deliberately looking at your environment as if you are seeing it for the first time. Safety professionals at companies like Total Energies Myanmar do not rely on gut feeling. They use checklists. A checklist forces your brain to re-notice what it has learned to ignore. The key behavior is not awareness — awareness fades. The key behavior is a repeatable process you execute every single day regardless of how you feel. Duke's principle applies here: celebrate the daily scan, not the hazard you found. The scan IS the success. Finding nothing means the scan worked and the environment is safe. Finding something means the scan worked and you prevented harm.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
How to scan a Myanmar workplace in 90 seconds

Here is the 6-point scan adapted for Myanmar manufacturing and office environments. Execute it every time you enter your workspace. First: Floor — is it wet, oily, cluttered, or uneven? Rainy season in Yangon means water tracks in from outside constantly. Second: Electrical — are wires exposed, overloaded power strips common, or sockets near water? With daily power outages and generator switchovers, electrical surges are a real and common hazard. Third: Lighting — can you see clearly at your workstation? Dim lighting causes both accidents and eye strain. Fourth: Exits — are emergency exits clear and unlocked? In Myanmar factories, exits are routinely blocked with inventory. Fifth: Chemicals — are containers labeled, lids closed, and stored away from heat? Garment factories use dyes and solvents that cause burns. Sixth: Overhead — are shelves stable, objects secured, nothing stacked above head height unsafely? This scan takes 90 seconds. Do it before you start work. Every day. The habit matters more than any single finding.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your phone camera right now and take a slow 30-second video panning across the room you are sitting in. Watch the video back. Pause every time you see something that could cause a slip, a burn, a shock, or a fall. Count them. Most people find 3 to 7 hazards in their own home they have never consciously noticed — exposed wires, unstable shelves, wet floors near power strips. This exercise trains the scanning habit you will use every day at work. Save this video in a folder called Safety Practice.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open Facebook and search for the page Myanmar Occupational Safety and Health. Follow it. Then search for the group Myanmar Industrial Workers Community. Read the three most recent posts about workplace incidents. For each one, write in your phone notepad: what was the hazard, how long did it likely exist before the incident, and who could have reported it. This builds your pattern recognition for real Myanmar workplace hazards — not textbook examples.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Download the free app Google Keep from Play Store if you do not have it. Create a new note titled My Hazard Checklist. Type these 6 items: floor condition, electrical wires and sockets, lighting level, emergency exit access, chemical container labels, heavy object storage height. Tomorrow when you arrive at work, spend 90 seconds mentally checking each item. Add a line to the note for anything you notice. This is your personal daily safety scan — the same process safety officers at an established Myanmar bank branches use.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Open Canva on your phone or go to canva.com in your browser. Search for the template called Safety Report. Look at how professional hazard reports are structured: photo, location, description, severity rating, date. Take a screenshot of one template you like and save it. You do not need to create a report today — you are building familiarity with the format so when you do report a hazard, it looks professional rather than like a casual complaint.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Open your phone notepad and write down the last 3 times you noticed something unsafe at work or in public and did nothing. For each one, write one sentence about why you stayed silent. Be honest. Common reasons include: not my job, someone else will handle it, I will look like a complainer. Read your three reasons back to yourself. These are the specific mental barriers you must overcome. Photograph this note and keep it — you will revisit it in Module 4.

Key Takeaway

The most dangerous hazard in any Myanmar workplace is not fire or chemicals — it is the hazard everyone has already normalized and stopped seeing.
