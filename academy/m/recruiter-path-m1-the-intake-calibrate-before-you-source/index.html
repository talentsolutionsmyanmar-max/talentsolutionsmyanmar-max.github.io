The storyCard 1 of 8
The Story

Ma Thiri is 24, in Insein, and just took her first real requisition off the ReferTRM board: "Sales Manager — a Yangon FMCG distributor — 1,000,000 MMK / month." She's thrilled. She knows salespeople; her cousin sells phones in Hledan. Within an hour she has twelve names — LinkedIn, her Viber groups, an old classmate — and she submits five strong-looking CVs the same afternoon. Two days later all five come back rejected. The hiring manager, U Kyaw Zin, is polite but tired: "None of these have run distribution. I need someone who has managed a route-sales team — vans, dealers, daily targets — not someone who sold handsets in a shop." Ma Thiri had never asked what the role actually does. She spent five submittals and a piece of her credibility on a picture of the job she invented in her own head. Here is the hard, freeing truth of this module: her search did not fail. Her ASSUMPTIONS were never calibrated. A great recruiter would have learned in one 20-minute conversation what Ma Thiri paid five rejections to find out.

The teachingCard 2 of 8
Why intake is the whole game

New recruiters think the job is finding people. It isn't — finding people is easy. The job is finding the FEW RIGHT people, and you cannot recognize "right" until you know exactly what the hiring manager means by it. That is what an intake does. Three ideas the best recruiters live by: (1) Your real score is your SUBMIT-TO-ACCEPT ratio — of the candidates you send, how many does the client agree to interview? Sending 20 and getting 1 interview is not hard work; it is noise. (2) Be a SNIPER, not a spammer. A sniper sends three to five precise submittals and most get accepted, because each one was aimed. (3) The clock that matters — time to your first good submittal — should start AFTER a thorough intake, never before. Sourcing before calibrating feels fast and is actually the slowest path, because every wrong submit costs you time, credibility, and the client's patience.

The teachingCard 3 of 8
The 5 Calibrations (run these before you source one name)
  1. ROLE REALITY — not the title, the day. Ask: "Walk me through what this person actually does in a normal week." Titles lie; days don't. "Sales Manager" can mean route-sales distribution, key-account selling, or retail floor — three different candidate pools. 2) THE THREE MUST-HAVES — force a short list. Ask: "If you could keep only three requirements and drop the rest, which three?" Everything else is a nice-to-have. This is the single highest-leverage question in recruiting. 3) THE 'GREAT' EXAMPLE — anchor on a real person. Ask: "Think of someone who did this job brilliantly. What made them great?" You are not sourcing a job description; you are sourcing a pattern the hiring manager already recognizes. 4) CONTEXT & CONSTRAINTS — why is the role open (growth? someone left? why did they leave?), the salary band and what's flexible, the team the person joins, and any deal-breakers. Unspoken deal-breakers kill more submittals than missing skills. 5) THE CALIBRATION CANDIDATE — do not source at scale yet. Find ONE or TWO profiles that seem to fit, send them early, and ask: "Is this the right direction?" One recalibration here saves ten wrong submittals later. This is how snipers aim. Output of the five: a one-page CALIBRATED SEARCH BRIEF — role reality in one sentence, the three must-haves, the 'great' pattern, the constraints, and your first calibration profile. That brief is your license to source.
The teachingCard 4 of 8
Running an intake with Myanmar hierarchy and face

Intake means asking a senior person a lot of direct questions — which can feel disrespectful if done bluntly. It isn't, if you frame it as service. Open with relationship and purpose: "Saya, so I bring you only the right people and don't waste your time, may I ask a few questions about the role?" Now your probing is a gift, not a challenge. Ask salary as a range, not a demand: "So I set the right expectation with candidates, what band are we working within?" When a manager is vague — common when the role is new or the manager is busy — do not fill the silence with your own assumptions; use the 'great example' question, which feels flattering rather than interrogating. And protect the candidate too: what you learn in intake is used to match honestly, never to pressure. Respect up (the manager) and respect down (the candidate) are the same skill.

The teachingCard 5 of 8
The reframe that keeps you in the game

You will run a perfect intake and still have searches that stall. When that happens, do not say "I failed the search." Say what a professional says: "What I'm finding is incongruent with our assumptions — let's recalibrate." This is not an excuse; it is a diagnosis. Nine times out of ten the market is telling you the must-haves and the salary band don't fit together, and the fix is a five-minute re-intake, not more sourcing. The recruiters who last are the ones who treat a stalled search as new information instead of personal failure.

The common mistakeCard 6 of 8
The mistake almost everyone makes

Sourcing before calibrating. It feels productive — you're "doing something" — but you're building speed in a random direction. Close cousins: taking the job TITLE at face value; never asking for a 'great' example; and never confirming the deal-breakers (location, shift, salary flexibility) that quietly disqualify half your submittals. If you remember one sentence: no name gets sourced before the Calibrated Search Brief exists.

Do thisCard 7 of 8
Your action steps (do this on a real req)
  1. Pick one open requisition from the board. 2) Book a 20-minute intake with the hiring manager (or use the intake message template if async). 3) Run the 5 Calibrations; write the one-page Calibrated Search Brief. 4) Get ONE calibration profile approved before sourcing at scale. 5) Only then source — and track your submit-to-accept ratio from your very first submittal.
