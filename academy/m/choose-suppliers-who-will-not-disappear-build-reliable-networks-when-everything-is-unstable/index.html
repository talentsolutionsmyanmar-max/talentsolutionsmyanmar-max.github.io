The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Su Mon coordinates purchases for a small hardware distribution company in South Dagon, Yangon. She has used the same steel supplier for three years — U Kyaw Tun, a friendly man she met through her uncle. They never signed a written agreement. Prices were discussed on Viber and confirmed with a handshake. In February 2025, U Kyaw Tun's business collapsed due to currency fluctuations. He disappeared overnight — no warning, no final delivery, no refund for the advance payment Ma Su Mon had sent. She lost 4.2 million kyats of company money. Her boss was furious but could not recover the funds because there was no written contract, no alternative supplier on file, and no documentation of terms. Ma Su Mon was not fired, but she was removed from purchasing duties and moved to data entry at half the responsibility. She had trusted a relationship instead of building a system. In Myanmar, relationships start the connection — but only systems protect it.

What do you already believe?

Think about how you or your company chooses suppliers. What matters most to you — is it the lowest price? The longest relationship? A trusted introduction? Be honest with yourself: if your main supplier disappeared tomorrow, do you have a backup ready? Write your honest answer. Your level of preparation reveals your level of professional risk.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

Myanmar workers choose and keep suppliers based almost entirely on personal relationships — family connections, introductions from respected elders, or simply 'we have always used them.' This is Emotional Reasoning — the cognitive distortion of believing that because the relationship feels trustworthy, it must be reliable. The cultural root cause runs deep in Myanmar: business operates on personal networks and 'ahnade' (the reluctance to cause discomfort), making it feel rude or disloyal to evaluate a long-term supplier or seek alternatives. The devastating consequence is that when that single supplier fails — and in Myanmar's volatile 2026 economy, many will — the worker who depended entirely on that relationship has no backup, no documentation, and no defense when their boss demands answers. They absorb the full blame for systemic failure they could have prevented.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Relationships Start Connections — Systems Protect Them

In Myanmar business culture, relationships are everything. An introduction from a respected person opens doors that cold calls never will. This is genuinely valuable — do not abandon it. But here is where workers go wrong: they confuse the relationship that starts a connection with the system that protects it. A good relationship means your supplier answers your call. A good system means you have written terms, documented performance, alternative contacts, and clear consequences if delivery fails. Think of it like this: a relationship is the roof of your house. A system is the foundation. Without the roof, rain comes in. But without the foundation, the entire house collapses. Nassim Taleb teaches asymmetric thinking: the cost of documenting your supplier relationships is small — an hour of work. The cost of not documenting is catastrophic — potentially millions of kyats and your job. Small effort, massive protection. Always take that trade. Celebrate the process: every time you write something down instead of relying on memory, you are building your professional foundation.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Building Supplier Resilience in Myanmar's Volatile Economy

Myanmar's 2026 economy creates specific supplier risks that other countries do not face. Currency fluctuations mean your supplier's costs can jump thirty percent overnight. Power outages mean production schedules are unreliable. Banking restrictions mean payments can be delayed or blocked. Political and economic instability means businesses close without warning. In this environment, single-supplier dependence is not just unwise — it is dangerous. Here is your practical approach: for every critical product, maintain relationships with at least two suppliers. You do not need to order from both — just keep contact warm. Send a WhatsApp message every month checking their prices and availability. When your primary supplier is late, you already have a phone number to call. In hierarchical Myanmar workplaces, present this to your supervisor as risk management, not disloyalty to existing suppliers. Frame it as: 'I want to make sure we are never stuck if something unexpected happens.' This framing protects face for everyone while demonstrating the strategic thinking that earns promotions at companies like a major conglomerate and a major multinational based in Yangon.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open Google Sheets and create a new sheet titled 'Supplier Scorecard.' Make six columns: Supplier Name, Product, Price Per Unit, Average Delivery Days, On-Time Delivery Percentage (estimate), Payment Terms. Fill in every supplier your workplace currently uses — even if you have to estimate some numbers. Most Myanmar workers have never written down their supplier information in one place. This single sheet transforms invisible knowledge into visible, shareable, manageable data. If a supplier disappears tomorrow, this sheet means you are not starting from zero.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open Facebook and search for wholesale or supplier groups related to your industry. For example, 'Myanmar Hardware Wholesale,' 'Yangon Food Suppliers,' or 'Myanmar FMCG Distribution.' Join at least two groups. Spend fifteen minutes scrolling through posts. Save or screenshot three potential alternative suppliers for products you currently buy from only one source. You are not switching suppliers — you are building options. Nassim Taleb's principle: never let yourself have zero alternatives. Having backup contacts is free insurance against catastrophic loss.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open your phone's document editor or Google Docs. Type a simple purchase order template in Myanmar language with these fields: Date, Supplier Name, Product Description, Quantity, Agreed Price, Delivery Deadline, Quality Condition (what counts as acceptable), and Signature Lines for both parties. Save this as a template. Under Myanmar Labor and Commercial Law, written agreements — even simple ones — provide vastly more legal protection than verbal promises. This fifteen-minute action could save millions of kyats. Send it to yourself on WhatsApp so you always have it accessible.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Think of your most important current supplier. Open your phone notes and answer honestly: Have they been late in the last three months? Have their prices increased more than inflation? Do they respond to your messages within 24 hours? Have they changed their payment terms suddenly? Each 'yes' to these questions is an early warning sign. Write them down. Now ask: if this supplier disappeared tomorrow, how many days would my company be unable to operate? If the answer is more than three days, finding a backup is not optional — it is urgent. Write the number of vulnerable days down. That number is your risk.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Send a WhatsApp message today to one person in your professional network asking: 'Who do you use for [your most critical product]? Are you happy with their reliability?' This is the simplest supplier research possible. In Myanmar, information about suppliers flows through personal networks — use that cultural strength intentionally instead of accidentally. Document every recommendation in your Supplier Scorecard sheet. Over time, you will build an alternative supplier database that makes you irreplaceable to any employer.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

A supplier relationship without documentation, backup options, and performance tracking is not loyalty — it is a single point of failure that can destroy your career in one phone call.
