Decide What Matters Most When Everything Feels Urgent and Resources Are Scarce

The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Thet Naung works at a small logistics company in Insein Township. Last month, on a single Monday morning, he faced five urgent requests simultaneously. His boss wanted a client report by noon. A driver called saying a truck broke down. The office printer jammed with invoices half-printed. A new client was waiting for a callback. And the internet had dropped so he could not access cloud files. Ko Thet Naung tried to do everything at once. He started the report, then ran to check the printer, then called the driver, then remembered the client and felt guilty, then tried to fix the internet. By noon he had completed nothing. Zero tasks finished. His boss saw an incomplete report and a missed client call. Ko Thet Naung was written up for poor performance. That night, he told his wife he felt like a failure. He was not a failure. He was a hardworking man who lacked one critical skill: the ability to decide what matters most when everything screams for attention. No one taught him this. Myanmar schools teach students to complete every assignment in the order given, not to choose which assignment matters most.

What do you already believe?

When you have five tasks due on the same day, how do you decide which to work on first? Do you work on whichever your boss mentioned most recently? Do you work on whichever feels easiest? Do you try to do all five simultaneously? Do you believe that a good worker should be able to handle everything at once, and that needing to choose means you are not capable enough?

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

Myanmar workers treat all tasks as equally urgent, attempting to work on everything simultaneously and finishing nothing completely. The cognitive distortion is All-or-Nothing Thinking: the belief that giving attention to one task means abandoning all others, so you must touch everything to be responsible. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's education system, which trains students to follow all instructions from authority without questioning order or relative importance. Additionally, in relationship-first workplace culture, refusing or delaying any request feels like personal rejection of the person who asked. The devastating consequence is chronic partial completion. Supervisors see someone who is always busy but never delivers anything finished. In MNC performance frameworks used by a major multinational based in Yangon and a major telecom operator, delivery of complete work outweighs visible busyness. Workers who cannot prioritize are judged as lacking management potential and are locked into junior roles indefinitely.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Why choosing one thing is harder and more valuable than doing everything

MJ DeMarco teaches that the progression from employee to leader starts with one skill: allocating scarce resources for maximum impact. Time is your scarcest resource. At work, you sell your time. Every hour you spend on a low-impact task is an hour stolen from a high-impact task. They are not equal. This is the concept most Myanmar workers miss because the education system rewards completing all assignments equally. But in a real workplace with real constraints, treating all tasks equally guarantees mediocre results on everything and excellent results on nothing. The Eisenhower Matrix, used by military commanders and Fortune 500 executives, sorts every task on two dimensions: Is it urgent? Is it important? Urgent means someone is waiting right now or something breaks today. Important means it affects your job, your client, or significant money. The magic insight: most tasks that feel urgent are not important, and most tasks that are truly important do not feel urgent. The email from a colleague asking about lunch plans feels urgent. Updating your skills to prepare for a promotion interview is important. Your instinct handles the email. Your discipline handles the skills update. Winners prioritize importance. Everyone else prioritizes whatever is loudest.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Prioritizing in Myanmar workplaces without offending anyone

The practical challenge in Myanmar is that refusing or delaying any task from a senior or colleague feels like personal rejection. In a culture where workplace harmony and face are paramount, saying I will do this later can sound like I do not respect you. This is why the yes-with-timeline approach is essential. You never say no. You never say later without context. You say: Yes, I will do it. Here is when I can deliver it based on my current commitments. This response accomplishes three things. It respects the relationship. It demonstrates that you are managing your workload professionally rather than chaotically. And it gives the other person the information they need to adjust their own timeline or escalate if truly critical. In practice, Myanmar workers who master this approach report that supervisors initially seem surprised but quickly come to respect the predictability. At companies like a mobile payments company and a major multinational based in Yangon, team leads specifically seek people who can articulate their capacity and manage their commitments visibly. The ability to say I am currently focused on X and can start your task at Y is a leadership signal in any organization. It shows you are thinking about the team's total output, not just reacting to the latest request. This is how junior workers get noticed for promotion into supervisory roles.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your Notes app right now. Write the heading: My Priorities Today. Below it, list every task currently on your plate at work. Do not organize them yet, just dump every single one. Now beside each task, write two letters. Write U if it is truly urgent, meaning someone is actively waiting or something breaks today if it is not done. Write I if it is truly important, meaning it affects your job security, a client relationship, or significant money. Tasks with both U and I go first. Tasks with only I go second. Tasks with only U go third. Tasks with neither go last or get dropped entirely. You have just built the Eisenhower Matrix on your phone in five minutes. This is the same framework taught in MBA programs worldwide.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Look at your prioritized list from step one. Circle or mark the single task at the top. This is your Number One. Open Google Keep or your phone calendar and create a morning event for tomorrow titled: Finish Number One Before Anything Else. Set it for the first hour of your workday. When you arrive at work tomorrow, work on this single task exclusively until it is finished or until you reach a point where you must wait for someone else. Only then move to the second task. This single-focus approach will feel uncomfortable because you are ignoring other things. That discomfort is the old All-or-Nothing Thinking pattern. Recognize it and continue.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open Google Sheets on your phone. Create a new sheet called: Daily Priority Log. Create four columns: Date, Number One Task, Completed Yes or No, and What I Learned. Every workday morning, fill in today's date and your number one task. Every evening, mark whether you completed it and write one short lesson. After two weeks of entries, you will see patterns: which types of tasks you consistently finish, which you avoid, and what blocks you most often. This log is your personal prioritization training program. It celebrates the process of choosing, not just the outcome of completing, following the Duke behavioral principle.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Draft a polite message template in your Notes app for when someone gives you a new urgent task while you are already working on your number one. Write: Thank you for this task. I am currently completing [current task] which is due at [time]. I can start on your request immediately after, which means I can deliver it by [realistic time]. Would that work? Save this template. It allows you to protect your focus without rejecting the person, which is essential in Myanmar's relationship-first workplace culture. You are not saying no. You are saying yes with a timeline. Practice reading this aloud so it sounds natural and respectful.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Every Sunday evening, set aside ten minutes for a Weekly Priority Preview. Open your project tracker from Module 3 and your calendar. Look at the week ahead. Identify the three most important tasks for the entire week, not the most urgent, the most important for your career, your team, and your company. Write these three tasks at the top of a new note titled: This Week's Top 3. Every morning, check if your daily Number One task aligns with one of these three. If your daily priorities consistently drift away from your weekly top three, you are being controlled by other people's urgency instead of your own importance. This weekly habit takes ten minutes and prevents months of wasted effort on low-impact work.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

Trying to do five things at once means finishing zero things well. The most productive worker is not the one who touches everything but the one who finishes the one thing that matters most, then moves to the next. Prioritization is not selfishness. It is the only honest response to limited time.

Think it overCard 12 of 12
How did this change what you knew?

Return to your original answer about how you handle five simultaneous tasks. Has your understanding shifted? Write down the one belief that changed the most. Was it the realization that multitasking produces zero completed tasks? Was it the permission to focus on one thing without guilt? Was it the technique of saying yes with a timeline? Identify the specific shift. This clarity about what changed is itself a skill: the ability to notice when you have learned something new, which accelerates all future learning.
