CORE INSIGHT

The expected salary question is not a trick -- it is a negotiation opening. Most Myanmar professionals treat it like a test with one correct answer. It is not. The employer is trying to find out two things: whether they can afford you, and whether you understand your own market value. When you answer well, you signal competence. When you answer poorly, you either price yourself out or leave serious money behind.

Here is what most people get wrong: they think the polite thing to do is say a low number. In Myanmar culture, humility is valued, and asking for too much feels aggressive. So a candidate earning 400,000 MMK says '450,000 or whatever you think is appropriate.' The employer, who budgeted 600,000 for the role, happily offers 480,000 and considers it generous. The candidate just lost 120,000 MMK per month -- that is 1,440,000 MMK per year -- because they confused politeness with self-sabotage.

The professionals who get this right do something different. They anchor with a researched range. Instead of a single number, they say: 'Based on my research and the scope of this role, I am looking at 550,000 to 650,000 MMK, depending on the full benefits package.' This does three things: it shows you did homework, it gives the employer room to negotiate, and it positions you at market rate rather than below it.

THE MYANMAR REALITY

Salary transparency barely exists in Myanmar. Unlike markets with Glassdoor data, you often cannot Google what a Supply Chain Coordinator at a Yangon distributor earns. This information asymmetry benefits employers. At local companies, salaries are rarely posted in job ads. NGOs and INGOs tend to publish salary bands, which makes this question easier. MNCs usually have internal grading systems, so the recruiter already knows the budget -- they are testing whether your expectation fits.

In 2026, this is shifting. Platforms like JobNet and Myanmar job Facebook groups increasingly show salary ranges. Some recruitment agencies, including firms like ReferTRM, share market data. AI tools like ChatGPT can help you draft salary research prompts. But the cultural challenge remains: many candidates, especially women, feel deeply uncomfortable stating a number. In a culture where a junior employee might say 'I will accept whatever the company decides,' knowing your number and saying it clearly is a genuine competitive advantage.

Gender matters here. Data from Myanmar recruitment consistently shows that women tend to state expected salaries 10-15% lower than men for equivalent roles. This is not because women value themselves less -- it is because cultural expectations around modesty hit harder. If you are a woman reading this, know that stating a market-rate range is not aggressive. It is accurate.

COMMON MISTAKE

The most damaging mistake Myanmar professionals make is saying 'negotiable' or 'up to the company' when asked about expected salary. They believe this makes them flexible and easy to work with. What it actually signals to an experienced HR manager is: this person has not researched the market, does not know their value, and can be offered the bottom of our budget. At a local manufacturing company in Hlaing Thar Yar, an HR head once told us: 'When candidates say negotiable, I always offer 15% less than budget. They always accept.' Do not be that candidate.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Your expected salary answer is not about being humble or aggressive -- it is about being informed. The candidate who researches the market and states a confident range earns more in one conversation than most people get from a year of hard work and hoping for a raise.
