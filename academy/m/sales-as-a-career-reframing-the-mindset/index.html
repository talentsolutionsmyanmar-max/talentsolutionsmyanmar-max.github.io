Sales as a Career — Reframing the Mindset

Sales is not a backup plan. It is not what people do when they cannot find a "real" job. Sales is the engine that drives every business in every industry in every country on earth. Nothing happens until someone sells something. The factory does not produce. The office does not open. The salary does not get paid. Sales makes it all possible.

In Myanmar, sales carries a stigma. Many people associate it with pushy street vendors, aggressive insurance agents, or network marketing schemes. Parents often tell their children to become doctors, engineers, or bankers — never salespeople. This cultural perception is both understandable and wrong. The stigma comes from seeing bad sales. Good sales is invisible. When a trusted advisor helps a company find the right supplier, that is sales. When a recruitment consultant matches the perfect candidate with the perfect job, that is sales. When a pharmacist recommends the right medicine for your symptoms, that is sales.

The career potential in sales is extraordinary. In Myanmar's growing economy, skilled salespeople are among the highest-paid professionals across every industry. A top-performing sales representative at an FMCG company can earn 800,000 to 2,000,000 MMK per month including commissions. A senior business development manager at a technology company can earn 2,000,000 to 5,000,000 MMK. These figures exceed what many engineers and accountants earn at the same experience level.

But the real value of a sales career goes beyond money. Sales teaches you skills that apply everywhere: how to listen deeply, how to handle rejection, how to build trust with strangers, how to negotiate, how to manage your time, how to set goals and track progress. These are life skills, not just job skills. A person who masters sales can succeed in any industry, any country, any economy.

The mindset shift required: stop thinking of sales as convincing people to buy things they do not need. Start thinking of sales as solving problems for people who need solutions. Your product or service exists because it solves a real problem. Your job is to find the people who have that problem and show them how your solution works. If your product does not solve their problem, do not sell it to them. This integrity is what separates a career in sales from a temporary job in sales.

Three questions to ask yourself: Do I genuinely believe my product helps people? Am I willing to learn and improve every single day? Can I handle hearing "no" 9 times to get 1 "yes"? If you answered yes to all three, sales is not your backup plan — it might be your best plan.

Real-World Scenario

When Ma Nwe Nwe told her parents she wanted to work in sales, her mother cried. "Sales people stand in the sun and beg strangers to buy things," she said. Ma Nwe Nwe joined Thura Group's B2B sales team anyway. Within two years, she was earning more than her engineering friends — because sales compensation in Myanmar's growing FMCG sector rewards results, not just credentials. Her mother stopped crying.

## Common Mistake

In Myanmar, sales carries stigma. Parents want their children to be engineers, doctors, or accountants — "respectable" professions. But the reality is that every company's survival depends on revenue, and revenue comes from sales. The highest-paid non-executive employees in most Myanmar companies are top salespeople. The mindset shift: sales is not about tricking people into buying things they do not need. Professional sales is about understanding problems and offering solutions.

## Action Steps

  1. Interview three people who work in B2B sales (not retail). Ask them: what do you actually do every day? How much do you earn? What skills matter most? Their answers will destroy stereotypes.
  1. Read one book on consultative selling (The Challenger Sale or SPIN Selling). These frameworks show that modern sales is problem-solving, not persuasion.
  1. Shadow a sales rep for one day. Most Myanmar companies will allow this if you ask. Seeing the reality is more powerful than reading about it.

## Reflection Question

Write down every negative belief you have about sales. For each one, ask: "Is this based on evidence or assumption?" Most sales stigma comes from outdated images of door-to-door hawkers, not from modern professional selling.
