Know Your True Farming Cost So No Middleman Can Cheat You

The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Zaw Myo is a rice farmer in Pyapon Township, Ayeyarwady Delta. He grows monsoon paddy on four acres his family has worked for three generations. Last November, a broker from Yangon offered him 520,000 kyat per hundred baskets. Ko Zaw Myo accepted immediately because the number sounded decent and last year he got only 480,000. He felt relieved. But when his wife Ma Hnin Si sat down with a notebook and added up seed cost, fertilizer, diesel for the pump, hired labor during transplanting, and the interest on their informal loan from the village money lender at 5% monthly, the total cost was 490,000 per hundred baskets. Their profit for five months of backbreaking work was 30,000 kyat — less than two days of construction labor in Yangon. Ko Zaw Myo had no idea he was essentially working for free because he had never once calculated his full input cost. The broker knew this. Every broker knows this.

The flipCard 2 of 12
What do you already believe?

Before we begin: When you sell your harvest, how do you decide whether the price offered is fair or unfair? Do you calculate your exact cost first, or do you compare to what other farmers got, or do you go by gut feeling? Be honest with yourself — most people have never been taught to question this.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

Most Myanmar smallholder farmers never separate personal expenses from farm expenses, and never include their own labor as a cost. The cognitive distortion is Mental Filtering — they see only the cash they receive at harvest and filter out the dozens of small costs accumulated over months. The cultural root cause is oral tradition economics: farming knowledge is passed through stories and habits, not spreadsheets, and asking elders about exact costs can feel disrespectful in Myanmar's hierarchy culture. The devastating consequence is generational: families farm for decades believing they are profitable when they are actually subsidizing middlemen with their own unpaid labor, making it impossible to invest in improvements or ever escape the cycle.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The invisible costs that eat your profit

Here is what decades of agricultural research worldwide confirms: most smallholder farmers underestimate their true costs by 30-50%. This is not because they are foolish. It is because farming costs are spread across months and feel small individually — 5,000 kyat for rope here, 15,000 kyat for a pump repair there. The human brain is wired to forget small scattered losses (this is called the Anchoring distortion — you anchor to the big visible number you receive at sale, not the many small numbers you spent). The process that changes everything is simple but powerful: record costs as they happen, not from memory after harvest. Even a note in your phone each time money leaves your hand for the farm. Morgan Housel's principle applies here — the farmer who consistently tracks beats the farmer with better land who does not track. The behavior of recording is more valuable than the knowledge of agronomy. Your first step is never better seeds or better fertilizer. Your first step is always knowing your numbers.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
How to use your cost number as a weapon in negotiation

In Myanmar's agricultural trading culture, the broker arrives at your village, names a price, and expects immediate acceptance. Saying no or negotiating feels uncomfortable — it risks the relationship, and you fear the broker will not return. This is face-saving culture working against you. But here is what changes when you know your exact cost: you are no longer guessing. When the broker says 520,000, and you know your cost is 490,000, you can calmly say 'I need at least 560,000 to make this season worthwhile for my family.' You are not being aggressive. You are being informed. In Myanmar's hierarchy-based negotiation, the person with information has quiet power. Brokers respect farmers who know their numbers because it signals this farmer cannot be exploited easily — and they will offer better prices to keep the relationship. Practice this: say your break-even number out loud three times. It should feel as natural as saying your name. That number is your shield.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your phone's built-in calculator or download the free app 'Calculator Plus' from Play Store. Write down every cost you remember from last growing season in your phone's Notes app. Start with these 8 categories: seed, fertilizer, pesticide, fuel or electricity for water pump, hired labor, land rent or tax, loan interest, and transport to buyer. Even rough estimates matter more than nothing. Save this note with the title 'My Farm Cost' and today's date.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open Facebook and search for the group 'Myanmar Rice Price Information' or 'Myanmar Agri Market Price' — these groups post daily wholesale prices from Bayintnaung Market in Yangon and other major trading hubs. Join at least two groups. Scroll back through the last 30 days of posts to see the price range for your crop. Screenshot the highest and lowest prices you find and save them in your phone gallery in a folder called 'Market Prices.'

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Using the cost list you created in Step 1, add everything together to get your total cost for the season. Then divide by the total baskets or viss you harvested last season. This is your cost-per-unit. Type this number into a new note titled 'My Break-Even Price.' Any buyer offering below this number is asking you to pay them from your own pocket. Memorize this number like you memorize your phone number.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Open YouTube and search 'Myanmar farm cost calculation' or 'စိုက်ပျိုးစရိတ် တွက်ချက်နည်း' — several Myanmar agricultural extension channels have 5-10 minute videos showing exactly how to track costs. Watch at least one video fully. Notice if they include any cost category you missed. Add it to your list.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Open WhatsApp or Viber and send your cost-per-unit number to one other farmer you trust — a friend, a relative, a neighbor. Ask them: 'Do you know your cost per basket?' This one conversation will do more than any training because it starts a habit of talking about numbers instead of just feelings about whether the season was good or bad.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

The farmer who cannot calculate their exact cost-per-unit is not actually a business owner — they are an unpaid laborer on their own land, and every middleman in the supply chain is designed to exploit exactly this blindness.
