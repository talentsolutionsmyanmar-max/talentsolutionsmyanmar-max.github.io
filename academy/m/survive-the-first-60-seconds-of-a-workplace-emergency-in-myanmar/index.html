The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Phyu Phyu is 26 and works the evening shift at a garment factory in South Dagon. On a Wednesday during monsoon season, the power went out as it does almost every day. The generator kicked in after 30 seconds. Then workers smelled burning plastic from the electrical panel near the cutting section. Smoke began filling the room. Ma Phyu Phyu froze. She looked around for her supervisor. The supervisor was not on the floor. Other workers were looking at each other, waiting for someone to tell them what to do. Twelve seconds passed. The smoke thickened. Finally one worker shouted to go to the front door. Everyone rushed to the main entrance — a single door. Workers crushed against each other. Ma Phyu Phyu was pushed into the door frame and bruised her ribs. The emergency exit at the back was clear and unlocked that day, but nobody went to it because nobody had ever practiced using it. The fire turned out to be small — an overloaded circuit — and was contained in minutes. But Ma Phyu Phyu's rib injury kept her from work for 10 days. In an emergency, the first 60 seconds determine everything. Ma Phyu Phyu had no plan for those 60 seconds.

The flipCard 2 of 12
What do you already believe?

Before reading further, think about this: if a fire started right now at your workplace, what would you do in the first 10 seconds? Do you know where your nearest exit is without looking? Do you know a second exit route in case the first one is blocked? If you are honest with yourself, you probably do not have a clear plan. Most workers believe they will figure it out in the moment. This module will show you why that belief is the most dangerous thing in your workplace.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The most common and most dangerous mistake Myanmar workers make about emergencies is the Normalcy Bias — the cognitive distortion where your brain assumes that because things have always been fine, this situation will also be fine. It is the voice that says this is probably nothing, someone else will handle it, I should wait and see. The cultural root cause is collective passivity in Myanmar group settings — workers look to each other and to authority figures for cues rather than taking individual action, reinforced by years of education that punished independent decision-making. The devastating consequence is measured in seconds. In fire emergencies, smoke density doubles approximately every 30 seconds in enclosed spaces. The 15 seconds workers spend waiting for someone to tell them what to do can be the difference between a safe evacuation and a crushing bottleneck at a single exit. Every second of hesitation is a second of escalating danger.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Why your brain betrays you in the first 10 seconds of an emergency

When an emergency begins, your brain does not shift into hero mode. It shifts into freeze mode. This is called the Normalcy Bias — a well-documented cognitive phenomenon where your brain, having experienced thousands of normal days, interprets the first signals of an emergency as probably normal too. The smoke is probably nothing. The alarm is probably a test. Someone else is probably handling it. Studies of building evacuations show that the average person waits 40 to 60 seconds after the first emergency signal before beginning to move. In a factory with smoke, those seconds are often the entire margin between safe evacuation and being trapped. The only counter to Normalcy Bias is pre-programmed response. When you have rehearsed a sequence — Alert, Orient, Move, Assist — your brain executes the program instead of defaulting to freeze. This is Duke's process principle at its most life-and-death application. You do not need bravery. You need a rehearsed sequence. Bravery without a plan produces chaos. A plan without bravery produces survival. Myanmar's daily power outages actually give you a training advantage — every outage is a moment to practice orienting toward your exits in sudden darkness.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
The Myanmar-specific emergencies you must prepare for differently

Three emergency types dominate Myanmar workplaces in 2026, and each requires a different first response. First: electrical fire from generator switchover. Myanmar's daily power cuts mean generators activate multiple times per day. Overloaded circuits and worn wiring cause electrical fires, especially during monsoon when water contacts electrical systems. For electrical fires, never use water. Move away from the electrical source, alert others, and evacuate. If your workplace has a fire extinguisher, it must be CO2 or dry chemical type — not water. Second: chemical exposure in manufacturing. Garment dyes, cleaning solvents, and food processing chemicals can spill or splash. Move upwind or to fresh air immediately. Remove contaminated clothing. Flush exposed skin with clean water for at least 15 minutes. Third: structural issues during monsoon. Heavy rain causes ceiling leaks, floor flooding, and weakened structures. If you see new cracks in walls, water coming through electrical fixtures, or sagging ceilings, evacuate the area immediately and report. Do not wait for permission. Each of these emergencies has a different correct first action. Memorize all three. Write them in your phone notepad under the heading Myanmar Emergency First Actions.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Stand up right now wherever you are. Look around and identify the two nearest exits from the room you are in. Time yourself walking calmly to each exit. Note which one is closer and which route has fewer obstacles. Now close your eyes and mentally walk each route from memory. Can you do it without opening your eyes? This exercise takes 3 minutes and it is the exact same spatial awareness drill that safety officers at factories in Hlaing Tharyar use during worker onboarding. Take a photo from your current position toward each exit and save them with the note: my two evacuation routes.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open your phone notepad and type the 60-Second Emergency Response Sequence. Memorize these 4 steps in order. Step 1 ALERT: Shout the emergency type loudly — Fire, Chemical Spill, or Earthquake. Do not assume others have noticed. Step 2 ORIENT: Turn toward your nearest pre-identified exit, not the main entrance. Step 3 MOVE: Walk fast, do not run. Stay low if there is smoke. Take nothing with you. Step 4 ASSIST: If the person next to you is frozen or confused, take their arm and pull them with you while moving toward the exit. Read this sequence 5 times. Then close the note and recite it from memory. Repeat until you can say all 4 steps without looking.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open Google Maps or any sketch app on your phone. Draw a rough map of your workplace floor showing your workstation, the main entrance, and any other exits including windows and back doors. Mark your two evacuation routes with arrows. If you cannot draw it from memory, that tells you something critical — you do not know your own workplace well enough to evacuate in smoke or darkness. Save this map on your phone home screen where you will see it daily. Update it if your workstation changes.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Search on YouTube for factory fire evacuation drill training. Watch one video that shows a real evacuation — not an animation. Notice three things: how fast the first person moves, how others follow, and where bottlenecks form. Then think about your own workplace. Where would the bottleneck be? Almost certainly at the main entrance because that is where everyone enters and so that is where everyone's brain directs them during panic. Write one sentence in your notepad: In an emergency at my workplace, I will go to [specific alternative exit] because the main entrance will be a bottleneck.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Open Viber or WhatsApp and send this message to one coworker you trust: I learned a 60-second emergency response sequence today. It has 4 steps: Alert, Orient, Move, Assist. Can I explain it to you tomorrow at break? Teaching someone else is the most powerful way to lock knowledge into your own memory. Research shows you retain 90 percent of what you teach versus 20 percent of what you hear. By teaching one person, you are also creating a second person in your workplace who will act instead of freeze. That single action could save both your lives in a real emergency.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

In a real emergency, you will not rise to the level of your courage — you will fall to the level of your preparation, and most Myanmar workers have zero preparation.
