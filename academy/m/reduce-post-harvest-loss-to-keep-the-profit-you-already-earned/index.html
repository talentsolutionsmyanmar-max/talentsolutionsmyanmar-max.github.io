Reduce Post-Harvest Loss to Keep the Profit You Already Earned

The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Thet Naing grows sesame near Magway, one of Myanmar's driest and hottest regions. In April 2025, he harvested 80 baskets from his two acres — a decent yield in a difficult year. He spread the sesame on a plastic sheet in his yard to dry, as his father taught him. But unseasonal rain came for two afternoons. He covered the sesame with another tarp, but moisture had already penetrated. By the time he sold two weeks later, the broker downgraded his sesame from Grade A to Grade B due to moisture content above 7%, cutting his price by 12,000 kyat per basket. Across 80 baskets, Ko Thet Naing lost 960,000 kyat — not from bad farming, not from low market prices, but from poor drying and storage in the fourteen days between harvest and sale. His wife Ma Khin Mar said it felt like someone stole from their house. In a way, someone did. The thief was preventable post-harvest loss.

What do you already believe?

If you wanted to increase your farming income by 20% next season, what would you invest in first — better seeds, more fertilizer, or something else? Most farmers answer seeds or fertilizer. Before reading further, ask yourself: how much of your harvest do you think you lose between the field and the buyer's scale, and do you consider this a significant or minor issue?

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

Myanmar farmers overwhelmingly focus investment and attention on the growing phase — better seeds, more fertilizer, pest control — while treating the period between harvest and sale as a passive waiting period requiring no skill or strategy. The cognitive distortion is the Sunk Cost Focus — because so much effort went into growing, the harvest feels like the finish line, and attention drops precisely when value is most vulnerable. The cultural root cause is traditional farming education in Myanmar, which is almost entirely about cultivation techniques passed from elders, with post-harvest handling treated as common sense not worth teaching. The devastating consequence: Myanmar's national post-harvest loss rate is estimated at 20-30% for rice and even higher for perishable crops, meaning farmers collectively lose billions of kyat in value that was already created.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The cheapest 20% income increase you will ever find

Here is a fact that should change how you think about farming economics forever: the Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that in Southeast Asia, post-harvest losses cost smallholder farmers more income than low yields do. Read that again. You can increase your effective income by 15-25% not by growing a single additional grain, but by preserving what you already harvested. This is Taleb's asymmetric thinking in action — a small improvement in storage (low cost, low effort) produces outsized income gains with zero risk. Compare this to buying expensive hybrid seeds (high cost, uncertain outcome, potential total loss if weather fails). The process principle from Duke applies: do not try to build perfect storage infrastructure overnight. Instead, improve one step each season. This season: better drying. Next season: hermetic storage bags. The season after: a raised platform. Each small improvement compounds. Celebrate each improvement as a behavior win regardless of final price received.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Practical post-harvest actions for Myanmar conditions

Myanmar's climate creates specific post-harvest challenges: high humidity in delta regions, extreme heat in the Dry Zone, and unpredictable rain during traditional drying periods. Here are approaches proven effective in Myanmar. For drying: never dry grain directly on bare ground — use clean tarpaulin raised on bamboo if possible, and turn grain every 2-3 hours. In Ayeyarwady Delta where humidity is high, consider two-stage drying. For storage: the biggest enemy is moisture re-absorption and insects. Hermetic bags (GrainPro, IRRI SuperBag) that seal completely are now available in Myanmar at agricultural supply shops in most towns — search Facebook Marketplace for suppliers near you. For timing: in Myanmar's culture, many farmers hold grain hoping prices will rise. This is rational only if your storage prevents quality loss. If grain quality degrades while you wait, the higher price per unit is offset by lower grade and lower weight. Know your storage capacity honestly before speculating on price movements.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open YouTube on your phone and search 'post-harvest loss Myanmar' or 'ရိတ်သိမ်းပြီး ဆုံးရှုံးမှု လျှော့ချနည်း' — the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) and FAO Myanmar have free videos in Myanmar language showing simple post-harvest techniques. Watch at least two videos. Focus specifically on drying and storage, which account for over 70% of all post-harvest loss. Note any technique that differs from what you currently do.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Search Google on your phone for 'moisture content target [your crop name]' — for rice it is 14%, for sesame 6-7%, for pulses 12-13%. Write this number in your Notes app. This is your quality benchmark. Then search 'how to test grain moisture without meter' — there are simple hand tests and bite tests that work surprisingly well. The salt test for rice (mix grain with salt in a jar, shake, if salt sticks the grain is too moist) takes zero investment and 2 minutes.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open Facebook and search for the group 'IRRI Myanmar' or 'Myanmar Post Harvest Network' or 'Proximity Designs Myanmar.' These organizations regularly post practical guidance on low-cost hermetic storage — sealed bags and containers that prevent moisture re-entry and insect damage. The GrainPro SuperBag costs around 3,000-5,000 kyat and can save 10-15% of stored grain value. Search 'GrainPro bag Myanmar buy' to find suppliers.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Calculate your personal loss: take your total harvest from last season in baskets or viss. Estimate what percentage you lost to damage, moisture downgrade, insect damage, or spoilage — even 10% is common. Multiply that quantity by your selling price. Write this number down. This is real money that you grew, that left your field, and that disappeared before reaching your pocket. Stare at this number. This is your motivation to change post-harvest practices.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Create a simple post-harvest checklist in your phone's Notes app with these items: (1) Check weather forecast for 3 days after planned harvest — search 'Magway weather' or your township name on Google. (2) Prepare clean, elevated drying surface before harvest day. (3) Test moisture before selling using hand test. (4) Store in sealed container or bag if not selling immediately. (5) Sell within optimal window, do not hold for speculative price increase beyond your storage capacity. Save this note and review it one week before every harvest.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

Growing more is not the path to higher income for most Myanmar farmers — losing less of what you already grew is faster, cheaper, and entirely within your control today without any new investment.
