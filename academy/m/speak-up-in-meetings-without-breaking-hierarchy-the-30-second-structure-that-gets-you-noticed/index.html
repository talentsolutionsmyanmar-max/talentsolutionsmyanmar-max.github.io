Speak Up in Meetings Without Breaking Hierarchy: The 30-Second Structure That Gets You Noticed

The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Thida Aye has worked at a food manufacturing company in Shwe Pyi Thar for three years. She is the most knowledgeable person on her team about quality control processes. But in every weekly meeting, she sits silently while Ko Min Khant — who joined six months ago but speaks confidently — gets praised by the department head. Last month, the company needed to send one person to a supplier audit in Thailand. Ma Thida Aye's manager chose Ko Min Khant. When Ma Thida Aye asked why, her manager said honestly: 'I do not know what you think because you never speak in meetings. I cannot send someone to represent our company if I have never heard them present an idea clearly.' Ma Thida Aye cried on the bus home to Insein. She knew the answer in every meeting. She had better ideas than Ko Min Khant. But knowledge locked inside your head has zero career value. Ko Min Khant earned per diem and a networking trip. Ma Thida Aye earned another year of invisibility.

The flipCard 2 of 12
What do you already believe?

Think about your last five work meetings. How many times did you share your opinion or idea aloud? If the answer is zero or one, ask yourself why. Do you believe it is not your place to speak as a junior person? Do you believe your manager already knows what you think? Do you believe speaking up could backfire? Hold that belief in your mind — we are about to examine whether it is protecting you or trapping you.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The number one mistake is staying completely silent in meetings and believing that good work will speak for itself. The cognitive distortion is the Fallacy of Fairness — believing that if you work hard and know your subject, managers should notice and reward you without you needing to speak up. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's hierarchy norm where junior staff are taught from childhood that speaking before elders or seniors is disrespectful, combined with the fear of saying something wrong and losing face publicly. The devastating consequence is permanent career stagnation. Managers cannot promote people they have never heard articulate an idea. In 2026, as MNC companies expand in Myanmar, verbal communication in meetings is weighted in every performance review cycle. Silence is not modesty — it is scored as lack of capability.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Why silence is the most expensive habit in your career

Let us name the distortion clearly: the Fallacy of Fairness. You believe that because you work hard and know your subject, fairness demands that your manager recognize this without you speaking. But workplaces are not fair in this way — they are attention economies. Managers evaluate what they can see and hear. Morgan Housel writes that financial success depends more on behavior than intelligence. Career success is identical: your behavior of speaking clearly in meetings matters more than your silent knowledge. The research from Duke's process-focused approach tells us something liberating: you do not need to say something brilliant. You need to say something structured. The PRE formula — Point, Reason, Example — takes thirty seconds. Thirty seconds of structured speech makes you more visible than thirty days of silent hard work. This feels unfair. It is unfair. But Taleb would say: stop fighting the reality of how workplaces evaluate people, and start using that reality to your advantage. One structured contribution per meeting. That is the asymmetric bet — tiny effort, massive potential career return.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Speaking up the Myanmar way: respecting hierarchy while being heard

The fear is real: in Myanmar culture, a junior person speaking boldly in front of seniors can be seen as disrespectful. This is not irrational — it is a real cultural risk. But there is a critical difference between challenging hierarchy and contributing within hierarchy. Challenging sounds like: 'I disagree with the manager's plan.' Contributing sounds like: 'Building on what our manager mentioned, I noticed that last week's data supports this direction because...' The second version adds your knowledge while reinforcing the senior person's authority. This is the permission-frame technique. You are not disagreeing — you are supporting with additional evidence. Other respect-safe phrases: 'May I add one point from my experience on the ground?' or 'I have seen something that might be useful for the team's decision.' Practice these in Myanmar language too. The key: every culture has ways to speak truth to power respectfully. Myanmar's version is to frame your contribution as service to the group's goal, not as personal opinion. Master this framing and you solve the hierarchy problem forever.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your phone's voice recorder app right now. You are going to practice the PRE formula: Point — state your main idea in one sentence. Reason — give one supporting reason. Example — give one brief example or fact. Try this: 'I think we should change our delivery schedule. The reason is that our current timing causes warehouse congestion every Monday. For example, last week three trucks waited over two hours.' Record yourself saying this. Play it back. You just spoke for about fifteen seconds. That is all you need in a meeting. Practice three more topics from your actual work.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open your Notes app and write down the agenda of your next work meeting — or if there is no formal agenda, write the three topics your team usually discusses. For each topic, draft one PRE contribution you could make. Write the exact words you would say. Having these written in advance removes the panic of thinking on the spot. Read each one aloud twice. You are not memorizing a script — you are training your brain to organize thoughts before your mouth opens. This preparation habit is how professionals at a major telecom operator and a major conglomerate prepare for every meeting.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Search YouTube on your phone for 'how to speak in meetings confidently' and watch one video under five minutes. While watching, notice that every confident speaker uses some version of structure — they never ramble. Screenshot the video title and save it. Now record yourself one more time using PRE on a work topic. Compare your second recording to your first. Improvement in tone and clarity after just two tries is normal. Celebrate the practice — that is the behavior that matters, as Duke's research shows: rewarding the process builds the habit.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Think of the most senior person you regularly have meetings with. Now write a respect-framed PRE opener for them. In Myanmar workplace culture, you can add a permission phrase before your point: 'If I may share an observation...' or 'I noticed something that might help the team...' Write three different permission phrases that feel natural to you in Myanmar or English. Save these in your Notes app under the heading 'Meeting Openers.' Having these ready means you never freeze trying to find the right respectful way to start speaking. The structure handles the respect — you handle the content.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Set a goal for your next meeting: speak once. Just once. Using PRE with a permission opener. After the meeting, open your Notes app and write what happened. Did you speak? How did it feel? How did people respond? Even if you chickened out, write that honestly. Then prepare again for the following meeting. Nassim Taleb's principle applies here: each small attempt has limited downside but massive potential upside. One good contribution in one meeting can change how your manager sees you permanently. The risk of silence is guaranteed invisibility. The risk of speaking is temporary discomfort.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

In your career, you are not paid for what you know — you are paid for what you can communicate that you know. Silent expertise is invisible expertise, and invisible expertise gets zero reward.
