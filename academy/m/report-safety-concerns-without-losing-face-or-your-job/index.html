The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Aung Kyaw is 31 and works at a cold storage warehouse operated by a food distribution company in Dagon Seikkan. For three weeks, he noticed that the emergency exit at the back of the warehouse was blocked by stacked pallets. Every day he walked past it. Every day he thought about saying something. Every day he decided not to because the pallets were placed there by U Than Oo, the senior warehouse supervisor who had worked there for 12 years. Ko Aung Kyaw had only been there 8 months. He imagined the conversation: U Than Oo would lose face, would think Ko Aung Kyaw was challenging his authority, might make his shifts miserable. So Ko Aung Kyaw stayed silent. During a quarterly safety audit by the company's regional manager, the blocked exit was discovered. The regional manager asked the whole team why nobody reported it. Everyone looked at the floor. Ko Aung Kyaw was not fired — but he lost the team leader promotion he had been informally promised. The regional manager later told HR: I cannot promote someone who sees danger and says nothing.

What do you already believe?

Before reading further, answer this: have you ever stayed silent about a safety concern at work because you were afraid of how your supervisor or a senior colleague would react? If yes, what exactly did you fear would happen — and did you ever check whether that fear was based on evidence or on feeling? Be specific with yourself about what the feared consequence was. This distinction between feeling-based fear and evidence-based fear is the core of this module.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The most destructive mistake Myanmar workers make with safety reporting is what psychologists call Emotional Reasoning — the belief that because speaking up feels dangerous, it must actually be dangerous. Workers feel fear about challenging a senior person and interpret that feeling as evidence that bad consequences will follow. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's deeply ingrained respect for age-based and position-based hierarchy, where questioning a senior is felt as a moral transgression, not just a workplace risk. The devastating career consequence is invisible: workers who stay silent are never considered for supervisory roles, safety committee membership, or MNC transfers. Employers cannot see your knowledge if you never demonstrate it. Your silence communicates that you lack judgment, initiative, and leadership capacity — the opposite of what you intended.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Emotional Reasoning: the invisible force that keeps Myanmar workplaces dangerous

Emotional Reasoning is a cognitive distortion identified by psychologists where a person believes something is true because they feel it is true. I feel afraid to report this, therefore reporting it must be dangerous. I feel that my supervisor will be angry, therefore my supervisor will be angry. This distortion is powerful in every culture but it is supercharged in Myanmar by the hierarchy norm. When a junior worker contemplates raising a concern about a senior person's decision, they feel a visceral sense of transgression — almost like a moral violation. That feeling is real. But the consequence they imagine is often not. Here is the asymmetric reality from Taleb's framework: the cost of reporting a hazard through proper channels with respectful language is usually very small — at most, a moment of mild discomfort. The cost of not reporting a hazard can be catastrophic — injury, death, legal liability, career damage. When you map the actual probabilities and consequences, the math overwhelmingly favors speaking up. The process to build this habit is rehearsal. You practice the words before you need them so that when the moment comes, you execute from preparation, not from emotion.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
The face-preserving reporting method that works in Myanmar hierarchy

Myanmar workplace culture requires a specific approach to safety reporting that Western training materials do not teach. The method is called Shared Ownership Framing. You never say: you created a hazard. You never say: this is wrong. You say: I noticed something that could affect our team and I want to help address it. This framing accomplishes three things simultaneously. First, it makes the hazard a shared problem rather than an accusation. Second, it positions you as a helper rather than a critic. Third, it gives the senior person the opportunity to take credit for fixing the issue — which actually motivates them to act. For written reports, the same principle applies. Write about the hazard, not the person. Write about the risk to the team, not the failure of management. Include your willingness to assist with the solution. At companies like an established Myanmar bank and a major conglomerate, safety committees specifically train this language because they know that accusatory reporting creates defensiveness and nothing changes, while collaborative reporting preserves face and gets hazards fixed. Your goal is not to be right. Your goal is to get the hazard removed. Collaborative framing achieves that goal in Myanmar far more effectively than confrontation.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your phone notepad and write three versions of a safety concern message. Version 1 for a peer: I noticed something at our station that could cause a problem — can we talk about it? Version 2 for a direct supervisor: I want to help us keep the team safe. I noticed [hazard] and wanted to bring it to your attention so we can address it together. Version 3 for HR or management: I am submitting this safety observation in writing as part of my commitment to workplace safety standards. The hazard is [describe] at [location]. Each version increases formality while maintaining face-saving collaborative tone. Save all three as a note titled Safety Report Scripts.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Go back to the note you made in Module 1 Step 5 where you wrote down three times you noticed something unsafe and did nothing. Read your reasons for silence again. Now for each reason, write: This was Emotional Reasoning because I felt [emotion] and treated it as a fact that [consequence] would happen. Then write: The evidence that I would actually lose my job for reporting this is [examine honestly]. Most workers discover they have zero evidence of actual punishment — only fear. This exercise from the Crosby framework breaks the distortion by forcing evidence examination.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open Google Keep and create a safety report template note. Include these fields: Date and Time, Location with specific detail, Hazard Description in 2 sentences, Category from Module 1 list, Photo attached yes or no, Reported to whom, Response received yes or no with date. Keep this template pinned in your app. When you identify a hazard, duplicate the template and fill it in. This creates a professional documentation trail that protects you legally and demonstrates initiative to any future employer who asks about your safety awareness.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Open Viber or WhatsApp and identify one trusted colleague. Send them this message: I have been learning about workplace safety reporting. I realized I have been staying silent about concerns because of hierarchy fears. Have you ever felt the same? Start a conversation about this. Research on safety culture shows that workers who have even one ally are 3 times more likely to report hazards. You are not building a protest group — you are building a support system that makes speaking up less frightening for both of you.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Search on YouTube for how to report workplace safety concern professionally. Watch one video, even a short one. Then open your phone camera and practice delivering your Version 2 script from Step 1 as if you were speaking to your actual supervisor. Record yourself. Watch it back. Notice your tone — is it accusatory or collaborative? Is it fearful or calm? Re-record until your tone sounds like a professional raising a legitimate concern, not a subordinate begging for permission. Save the best version. This rehearsal technique is used by safety trainers at international companies operating in Myanmar.

Key Takeaway

In Myanmar's hierarchy culture, silence about safety feels like respect — but every MNC employer interprets that silence as a disqualifying lack of initiative.
