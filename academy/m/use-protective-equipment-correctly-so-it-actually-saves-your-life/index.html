The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Zin Mar works in a food processing plant in Shwe Pyi Thar industrial zone. The company provides hairnets, gloves, and safety shoes. Ma Zin Mar wears all three every shift. But she wears her gloves with the cuffs folded down because they are uncomfortable. She tucks her hairnet loosely because pulling it tight gives her a headache. She leaves her safety shoes unlaced because they are a size too large. Last month, hot oil splashed from a fryer and ran under her folded glove cuff, burning the inside of her wrist badly. She was wearing the PPE. It did not protect her because she was wearing it wrong. Her supervisor wrote in the incident report that Ma Zin Mar was provided with appropriate equipment. The implication was clear: if you were wearing the equipment and still got hurt, the fault is yours. Ma Zin Mar lost two weeks of wages and paid 60,000 kyat for burn treatment. She thought wearing PPE was enough. She never learned that how you wear it determines whether it works.

What do you already believe?

Think about this honestly: when you put on safety gloves or a mask at work, do you check whether they fit correctly — or do you just put them on because you are supposed to? Do you believe that simply wearing PPE protects you, or do you think the way you wear it changes how much protection you get? Most workers have never been asked this question. Your answer reveals whether your PPE is protecting you or just decorating you.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The number one PPE mistake among Myanmar workers is what safety professionals call compliance theater — putting equipment on to satisfy a supervisor's visual check without ensuring it actually provides protection. The cognitive distortion is Illusion of Control — the false belief that wearing PPE in any fashion creates safety, the same way wearing a seatbelt loosely creates the feeling of being buckled in. The cultural root cause is that Myanmar workplace training overwhelmingly emphasizes rule following over understanding. Workers are told to wear the gloves but not why the glove cuff must cover the wrist or what happens when it does not. The devastating consequence is injuries that occur while technically wearing PPE, which employers then use to shift blame entirely onto the worker, denying compensation and damaging the worker's employment record.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The difference between wearing PPE and being protected by PPE

There is a critical gap between having PPE on your body and having PPE that functions as protection. A mask with gaps at the nose lets in 40 to 60 percent of airborne particles. A glove with a folded cuff channels liquids directly onto the skin it was supposed to protect. Safety shoes that are too large shift during movement and can cause trips. Every piece of PPE has what engineers call a critical fit point — the specific place where proper contact with your body creates the protective barrier. Miss that point and the PPE becomes what Morgan Housel would call a false signal: it tells your brain you are safe, so you take more risks, while providing less protection. This is worse than wearing nothing, because without PPE you would at least be cautious. The process that matters is not putting PPE on — it is checking the fit every time. Duke's principle: celebrate the fit check, not the wearing. The check IS the safety behavior.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Solving the fit problem in Myanmar workplaces where PPE is one-size-fits-all

A real challenge in many Myanmar factories is that employers provide PPE in limited sizes. Gloves are too large for smaller hands. Masks gap on narrower faces. Safety shoes come in men's sizes only. This is not an excuse to wear ill-fitting PPE — it is a problem you must solve. First, for gloves that are too large, tuck the excess material at fingertips inward and ensure the cuff is extended fully. For masks, use the nose wire aggressively — press it firmly around your nose bridge and check for air leaks by cupping your hands over the mask and exhaling. For shoes, wear thicker socks or use an insole. Second, document the fit problem. Take a photo showing the gap or the excess material. Show it to your supervisor using the collaborative script from Module 2: I want to make sure my PPE protects me properly. Can we find a size that fits better? This is not complaining. Under the 2013 Factories Act, the employer's obligation is to provide appropriate protective equipment — appropriate includes proper fit. Third, if no better size is available, your documentation creates a record that protects you if an injury occurs despite you wearing the provided PPE.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open YouTube on your phone and search for PPE correct wearing technique manufacturing. Watch at least two videos — look specifically for the fitting step, not just the putting-on step. Notice how in professional demonstrations they always check the seal of a mask by breathing in and feeling for air leaks, check glove cuffs extend past the wrist, and ensure shoes are laced fully. Screenshot or save the best video to your phone for reference. Even on 3G, a 3-minute safety video will load and could save your hands or your eyes.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Find any pair of gloves in your home — kitchen gloves or cleaning gloves work. Put them on. Now fold the cuff down to your wrist like many factory workers do. Pour a small amount of water slowly toward your wrist from above. Watch how the water flows under the folded cuff and pools inside the glove against your skin. Now unfold the cuff so it extends past your wrist and repeat. See the difference. Take two photos showing both results. Save them with the note: this is why cuff position matters. You just proved through direct experience what a lecture could never teach you.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open your phone notepad and create a note titled My PPE Fit Check. Write this checklist: Gloves — cuffs past wrist, no gaps at fingers. Mask — nose wire pressed tight, no air leaking past edges. Hairnet — all hair inside, net sits at forehead not above it. Shoes — laced fully, correct size no sliding. Eye protection — sits flat against face, no gaps at temples. Read this checklist every morning before you put on your equipment at work. The 20 seconds this takes can prevent the injury that costs you 2 months of wages.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Open your phone camera and record a 60-second video of yourself putting on any available PPE or simulated PPE — kitchen gloves, a cloth mask, covered shoes. Narrate in Myanmar what you are checking at each step: I am checking the glove cuff extends past my wrist, I am pressing the mask nose wire to create a seal. Watch the video back. This self-recording technique is used in professional safety training. You are building muscle memory and verbal fluency that will make you stand out in any workplace safety discussion or interview.

Key Takeaway

PPE worn incorrectly is not safety equipment — it is a costume that gives you false confidence to take risks you would otherwise avoid.
