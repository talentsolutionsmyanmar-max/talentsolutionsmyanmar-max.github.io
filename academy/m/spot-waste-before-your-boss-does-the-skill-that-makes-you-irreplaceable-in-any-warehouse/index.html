The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ko Min Htet works night shift at a cold storage facility near Shwe Pyi Thar Industrial Zone. Every night, the power cuts for two to four hours. The backup generator takes twenty minutes to start. During those twenty minutes, temperatures in the chilled section rise. Ko Min Htet noticed condensation on chicken packaging every morning after outages — a sign the cold chain had been broken. He mentioned it to his coworker, who shrugged and said 'it is always like this, nothing we can do.' Ko Min Htet said nothing more. Three months later, a batch of frozen seafood was rejected by a hotel client — bacterial count was too high. The company lost 7.6 million kyats and the hotel contract. When management investigated, they found no temperature logs, no incident reports, no documentation of power outage impacts. Ko Min Htet had seen the problem every single night for ninety days. He had the knowledge to prevent a catastrophe but believed waste was someone else's responsibility. His silence cost his company a major client and cost him the trust of his managers.

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

Myanmar supply chain workers treat waste — damaged goods, expired products, rejected shipments, energy loss during power outages — as normal and inevitable. This is Learned Helplessness, the cognitive distortion of believing that because a problem has always existed, it cannot be changed. The cultural root cause is twofold: first, Myanmar's rote education system trains workers to accept conditions rather than question them; second, hierarchical workplaces punish the messenger who reports problems, so workers learn silence as a survival strategy. The devastating consequence is that workers who normalize waste are seen by MNC employers as part of the problem, not the solution. Companies like Total Myanmar Energy and a major telecom operator explicitly screen for 'continuous improvement mindset' in interviews. Workers who say 'waste is normal here' are revealing that they lack the single most valuable skill in modern supply chain management.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
Waste Is Not Normal — It Is Unmeasured

Here is a truth that will change how you see your workplace: waste does not persist because it is inevitable. It persists because nobody measures it. When a product expires on a shelf, someone paid for it. When a delivery is rejected for damage, someone paid for the transport. When electricity runs a generator during an outage, someone pays for the diesel. Every waste event has a kyat value. The reason it continues is that no one assigns that number. The moment you write '3 cartons of noodles damaged from moisture this week, value 45,000 kyats,' waste stops being background noise and becomes a visible, fixable problem. This is the core of Lean thinking adapted for Myanmar reality. You do not need expensive consultants or Japanese methodology books. You need a phone, a spreadsheet, and the habit of writing down what everyone else walks past. Celebrate the process: every waste event you log is a rep in your professional development. You are training your eye to see what untrained workers are blind to. Duke's principle applies perfectly — the logging habit matters more than the immediate result.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
How to Report Waste in Myanmar's Hierarchical Workplace Without Losing Your Job

The biggest barrier to waste reduction in Myanmar is not technical — it is cultural. Workers fear that reporting waste will be seen as criticizing their seniors. In many workplaces, the person who mentions a problem becomes the person blamed for it. This is real. Do not ignore this fear — navigate it. Strategy one: make it about data, not blame. Never say 'we are wasting too much.' Say 'I tracked our packaging damage this month and found some interesting numbers. Would you like to see?' Strategy two: connect waste reduction to your boss's goals. If your boss cares about profit, frame waste as lost profit. If your boss cares about impressing their superior, frame waste reduction as something that will look good in their report. Strategy three: start absurdly small. Do not propose a company-wide system. Propose fixing one waste problem in one area for one month. Success builds permission. In MNC environments like a major multinational based in Yangon, waste reporting is formally valued. In local Myanmar companies, it requires diplomatic skill. Both contexts reward the worker who sees waste as opportunity rather than fate. That worker is rare. Be that worker.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open Google Sheets and create a 'Waste Log' with these columns: Date, Product Affected, Type of Waste (Damage, Expiry, Rejection, Delay, Other), Estimated Quantity Lost, Estimated Kyat Value, Probable Cause. For one week, log every waste event you observe — even small ones. A dented can counts. A delayed shipment counts. Expired labels count. Most workers have never quantified waste. When you see the weekly total in kyats, the urgency becomes impossible to ignore. This is Taleb's principle: small tracking actions reveal hidden catastrophic risks.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Take your phone camera and photograph three areas in your workplace or a familiar market where you can see visible waste — damaged packaging, expired products on shelves, goods stored in rain or sun exposure, overcrowded storage causing crush damage. Do not show these photos to anyone yet. Save them in a folder called 'Waste Evidence.' These photos are data. When you eventually present a waste reduction idea to your supervisor, visual evidence is ten times more persuasive than verbal complaints. You are building a case, not making a complaint.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Open YouTube and search 'seven types of waste lean manufacturing simple.' Watch one video under ten minutes. As you watch, write in your notes app which types of waste you personally witness at your workplace. Most Myanmar workers will recognize at least four of the seven types happening daily. The power of naming waste is that once you can categorize it, you can measure it. Once you can measure it, you can reduce it. Once you can reduce it, you become the worker every manager wants on their team.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Open your phone calculator and do this rough calculation for one waste type: estimate how many units are damaged or lost per month. Multiply by the unit cost. Multiply by twelve months. Write down the annual figure. Most workers are shocked — a small daily waste of ten units at 2,000 kyats each equals 7.2 million kyats per year. When you present waste in annual kyats rather than daily units, the scale becomes visible to management. This reframing — from small daily normal to large annual crisis — is the communication skill that gets waste reduction budgets approved.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Draft a WhatsApp message (do not send yet) to your supervisor using this exact structure: 'I noticed that [specific waste event] has been happening approximately [frequency]. I estimate it may cost about [kyat amount] per month. I have an idea that might reduce it: [your simple idea]. Would it be appropriate for me to share more details?' This structure respects hierarchy (asks permission), presents data (not emotion), offers a solution (not just a problem), and protects face (uses 'may' and 'approximately' instead of absolute claims). Save this draft. Edit it as you collect more data this week. Send it when your numbers are solid.

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

Waste is not a force of nature — it is a series of small, visible warning signs that someone chose not to act on. The worker who documents waste before it becomes a crisis is worth ten workers who only report problems after the damage is done.
