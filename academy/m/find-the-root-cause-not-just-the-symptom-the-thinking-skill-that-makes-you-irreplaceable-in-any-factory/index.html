The storyCard 1 of 12
The Story

Ma Su Myat works in a garment factory in Hlaing Tharyar producing jackets for a European brand. For three weeks, she keeps finding the same defect: loose buttons on the left chest pocket. Every day she rejects 30 to 40 jackets for this issue. Every day the production team resews the buttons. Every day the same defect appears again. Ma Su Myat is exhausted. Her reject rate report looks terrible. Her supervisor tells her to 'inspect more carefully.' But Ma Su Myat IS inspecting carefully — that is how she keeps finding the defects. The real problem is that nobody is asking WHY the buttons keep coming loose. After three weeks of this cycle, the European buyer cancels the repeat order worth 150 million kyat. At the post-mortem meeting, a visiting QC consultant discovers the answer in five minutes: the button-sewing machine's thread tension cam was worn down by 0.4mm, causing inconsistent tension on every eighth stitch cycle. The fix cost 15,000 kyat for a replacement part. Ma Su Myat had spent three weeks catching symptoms of a problem that a single question — 'why does this keep happening?' — could have solved on day one.

What do you already believe?

When you find the same defect appearing repeatedly in a factory, what do you believe the correct response is? Most Myanmar QC workers believe the answer is 'inspect more carefully' or 'reject more strictly.' Do you believe your job is to catch defects, or to prevent them from happening? Where do you think defects actually come from — careless workers, bad machines, poor materials, or something else?

The common mistakeCard 3 of 12
The Common Trap

The number one mistake is treating recurring defects as individual events instead of symptoms of a systemic cause. The cognitive distortion is Anchoring — fixating on the visible defect (the loose button, the surface scratch, the wrong measurement) without questioning the underlying system that produces it repeatedly. The cultural root cause is Myanmar's rote education system that rewards finding the right answer, not asking the right question. Workers are trained to detect and remove, not to investigate and prevent. Additionally, Myanmar's factory hierarchy discourages line workers from questioning production processes — that is considered the engineer's or supervisor's domain. The devastating consequence: the factory stays trapped in a costly detect-reject-rework cycle, the worker's reject rate statistics look bad, and when the buyer asks 'what corrective actions did you take?' nobody has an answer beyond 'we inspected more carefully.' Buyers leave.

The teachingCard 4 of 12
The 5-Why Method: The Most Powerful Tool That Costs Nothing

Toyota invented the 5-Why method in Japan, and it transformed manufacturing worldwide. The principle is simple: when you encounter a problem, ask 'why?' five times. Each answer peels back a layer until you reach the systemic root cause. Example from a Myanmar garment factory: Problem — jacket zippers are misaligned. Why 1: The zipper tape was sewn at an angle. Why 2: The operator's guide rail was loose. Why 3: The guide rail screws were stripped. Why 4: The screws were replaced with wrong-size substitutes during last month's repair. Why 5: The maintenance team does not have a parts specification list and uses whatever fits. Root cause: missing maintenance documentation, not operator carelessness. Duke's process-first principle is essential here: the value is in DOING the 5-Why analysis consistently, not in whether your first attempt reaches the perfect root cause. Celebrate the asking. The quality of your answers improves with practice. What never improves is the approach of 'just inspect harder' — that is treating cancer with painkillers.

The teachingCard 5 of 12
Using Root Cause Thinking to Advance Your Career in Myanmar Factories

Here is the career reality in Myanmar manufacturing: factories have hundreds of workers who can inspect. They have very few who can think analytically about WHY defects happen. When an MNC buyer audits a factory, they always ask: 'What corrective actions have you taken for recurring defects?' The factory that answers 'we trained our operators to be more careful' fails the audit. The factory that answers 'we performed root cause analysis, identified the thread tension cam was worn, replaced it, and monitored the defect rate — it dropped from 7.5% to 0.3% — here is the documentation' passes with honors. You can be the person who provides that answer. You do not need to be an engineer. You need the 5-Why method, a phone camera for evidence, a Google Sheets log showing the defect trend before and after your intervention, and the confidence to present it. In MJ DeMarco's framework, this is the leap from employee-thinking to creator-thinking. You stop being the person who follows instructions and become the person who improves the system. This is how QC inspectors at 350,000 kyat become QA managers at 800,000 to 1,200,000 kyat in Myanmar's 2026 market — not through seniority, but through demonstrated analytical thinking.

Do thisCard 6 of 12
Action 1

Open your Notes app and practice the 5-Why method right now with a simple example. Start with a problem: 'The rice I cooked today was too soft.' Ask Why 1: 'Why was it too soft? Because I added too much water.' Why 2: 'Why did I add too much water? Because I estimated instead of measuring.' Why 3: 'Why did I estimate? Because I could not find the measuring cup.' Why 4: 'Why could I not find it? Because the kitchen is disorganized.' Why 5: 'Why is the kitchen disorganized? Because we have no designated storage system.' The root cause of soft rice is not water — it is storage organization. Do this exercise with 3 household problems. The thinking pattern transfers directly to factory defects.

Do thisCard 7 of 12
Action 2

Open Canva on your phone. Search templates for 'fishbone diagram' or 'cause and effect diagram.' Select a simple template. Label the head as a common factory defect: 'Button comes loose.' Label the four main bones: Machine (thread tension, needle condition), Material (thread quality, button type), Method (stitch pattern, operator technique), Manpower (training level, fatigue, shift timing). Fill in at least 2 potential causes per bone. Save and screenshot. This visual tool is used in every serious QC meeting worldwide. Having one on your phone ready to show proves analytical thinking that impresses any MNC interviewer.

Do thisCard 8 of 12
Action 3

Search YouTube for '5 Why root cause analysis manufacturing example.' Watch two videos, preferably ones showing factory examples. Pause each video when they reveal the root cause and check whether you guessed it correctly. Notice how the root cause is almost never the obvious first answer. Write down in your Notes app the three most surprising root causes you learn from these videos. This builds your intuition for looking past symptoms. Many videos have Myanmar-language subtitles or you can use auto-translate.

Do thisCard 9 of 12
Action 4

Think about one recurring problem in your current or most recent workplace — or a problem you have observed at any business. Write the full 5-Why chain in your Notes app. Then write a corrective action recommendation using this professional format: 'Problem: [state it]. Root Cause: [from your 5-Why]. Corrective Action: [what should change in the system]. Verification: [how will we know it worked].' This four-line format is standard in ISO 9001 quality management systems. Practice writing three of these. Each one adds to your professional portfolio.

Do thisCard 10 of 12
Action 5

Create a WhatsApp voice note — just for yourself, not to send — where you explain one root cause analysis you performed, in under 2 minutes. Speak as if you are presenting to a factory manager. Use clear, factual language. Play it back and listen for filler words, vague language, or unclear logic. Re-record until it sounds professional and confident. This verbal practice is critical because in Myanmar factory interviews and audits, you will be asked to explain your QC thinking verbally. The worker who can articulate root cause analysis fluently stands out dramatically from one who can only say 'I check the product carefully.'

Keep thisCard 11 of 12
Key Takeaway

A QC inspector who only finds defects is a cost center the factory tolerates. A QC inspector who finds WHY defects happen is a strategic asset the factory cannot afford to lose. The difference is one question asked five times.

Think it overCard 12 of 12
How did this change what you knew?

At the beginning, you likely believed that a QC inspector's job is to find and reject defective products. Has your understanding expanded? Can you now see that finding the root cause of defects is a completely different — and far more valuable — skill than finding the defects themselves? Write down one recurring problem from your life or work and commit to performing a 5-Why analysis on it this week. The shift from symptom-thinking to system-thinking is the single most valuable mental upgrade this entire training offers.
